Stochastic Oscillator Explained: %K, %D and Crosses

The stochastic answers a simple question — where is price sitting inside its recent range? Here is how the %K and %D lines work, what the 80/20 zones really mean, and where the stochastic sits in the CPE Trade Pro momentum score.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

The stochastic oscillator is one of the oldest momentum tools on the chart, and one of the most literal. It does not try to be clever: it measures where the current close sits between the highest high and the lowest low of a chosen window, and shows that as a percentage. That simplicity is why it is worth understanding properly.

What the stochastic measures

The calculation is straightforward. Over the last N periods, take the highest high and the lowest low. The %K line is how far the close is up that range, expressed from 0 to 100:

%K = (close − lowest low) ÷ (highest high − lowest low) × 100

A reading near 100 means the close is at the top of the recent range; a reading near 0 means it is at the bottom. A second line, %D, is a short moving average of %K, which smooths the signal and creates the crosses traders watch. This makes the stochastic a range indicator — it is most useful when a market is moving within a range, and can stay pinned at an extreme when a strong trend runs.

%K and %D (figure)

%K is the faster line, drawn as the raw reading. %D is the slower line, a 3-period average of %K by default. When %K and %D cross, one is overtaking the other — the faster line changing the short-term balance.

The 80 / 20 zones

Readings above 80 are called overbought and below 20 oversold, and the same warning that applies to RSI applies here: these are descriptions, not instructions. Above 80 simply means the close has been near the top of its recent range — which is exactly what a strong uptrend looks like. Below 20 is what a strong downtrend looks like.

The zones become genuinely useful when they coincide with a level: a dip into the lower zone right at support, or a push into the upper zone into resistance. The zone tells you the short-term range is stretched; the level tells you whether that matters.

⚠️
Extremes persist in trends In a strong trend the stochastic can sit above 80 or below 20 for a long stretch. Fading every extreme is a common way to lose money — wait for the zone to align with structure.

Crosses in the right region (figure)

A cross is %K passing through %D. A bullish cross is %K crossing up through %D; a bearish cross is the reverse. Crosses happen constantly, and most are meaningless because they occur mid-range where nothing is stretched.

The ones worth attention are zonal: a bullish cross while both lines are in or rising out of the lower zone, or a bearish cross while both are in or falling from the upper zone. That is where the range reading and a turn have the best chance of meaning something.

Stochastic vs RSI

Both are bounded momentum oscillators on a 0-100 scale, and both have overbought/oversold zones, so they are often confused. The difference is what they measure. RSI compares the size of gains to losses; the stochastic compares the close to the recent range. The stochastic is faster and more prone to extremes, which makes it good for timing inside a range and noisy in a trend.

Settings (14, 3, 3)

The classic trio of numbers controls the stochastic:

  • 14 (length). How many periods define the high-low range. Shorter = twitchier; longer = calmer.
  • 3 (%K smoothing). How much the raw %K is averaged. This is what turns a fast stochastic into a slow one — and the slow version is the one most traders actually use.
  • 3 (%D). The smoothing applied to %K to produce the %D signal line.

Fast vs slow is the main choice. The fast stochastic reacts first but is noisy; the slow stochastic smooths %K once more and produces fewer, cleaner crosses at the cost of a little lag. When in doubt, start with the slow version.

Common mistakes

  • Trading every cross. Mid-range crosses are noise — location is what matters.
  • Fading extremes in a trend. The stochastic can stay pinned; that is strength, not a reversal.
  • Ignoring the range context. The indicator is a range tool and behaves poorly in a one-way market.
  • Using it for direction. It measures position in a range, not the trend.
  • Stacking too many averages. Over-smoothing removes the speed that is the stochastic's whole point.

Ichimoku Cloud explained: lines, cloud and signals

Ichimoku Kinko Hyo is a whole trend system in one overlay — five lines and a shaded cloud that together answer trend, support, resistance and momentum. Here is what each piece does, without the mystique.

10 min read Updated 2026 Category Indicators

Ichimoku Kinko Hyo is one of the few indicators that tries to do everything at once. Its name roughly translates as "one glance equilibrium chart" — the idea being that a single look should tell you the trend, the momentum, and where support and resistance sit. It looks busy the first time you switch it on, but it is built from just five moving-average lines, and each has one clear job.

This guide explains what the five lines are, how the cloud is formed, how to read price against it, and how the whole overlay feeds into CPE Trade Pro's analysis.

1. The five lines

Everything in Ichimoku is a moving average of a high or a low over some period, then a few of them are shifted forward or backward. The five components are:

  • Tenkan-sen — the conversion line. The midpoint of the highest high and lowest low over the last 9 periods.
  • Kijun-sen — the base line. The same midpoint over the last 26 periods. Slower than the Tenkan.
  • Senkou span A — the average of the Tenkan and Kijun, plotted 26 periods ahead of price.
  • Senkou span B — the midpoint of the 52-period high and low, also plotted 26 periods ahead.
  • Chikou span — today's close plotted 26 periods behind price.

The two Senkou spans are drawn in the future, and the shaded area between them is the Kumo — the cloud. The Chikou is drawn in the past. That forward and backward shift is what makes Ichimoku look unusual, and it is intentional: the cloud projects support and resistance ahead of price.

2. Tenkan and Kijun

The Tenkan and Kijun are the two lines you read first. The Tenkan is the faster of the two; the Kijun is the slower reference. Their relationship mirrors the fast/slow pairing you find in EMA crosses.

When the Tenkan is above the Kijun, the shorter-term midpoint is higher than the longer one — a lean toward bulls. When the Tenkan crosses the Kijun, it marks a shift in that balance. The Kijun on its own is also watched as a support and resistance line: price pulling back to the Kijun and turning is a common continuation signal.

ℹ️
Midpoints, not averages of closes Ichimoku lines use the midpoint of the high–low range, not the closing price. That is why they often sit differently from a plain moving average.

3. The cloud (Senkou spans A and B)

The cloud is the signature feature. Senkou span A is the average of the Tenkan and Kijun; Senkou span B is the 52-period midpoint. Both are plotted 26 periods into the future, and the space between them is filled in.

The cloud's colour tells you which span is on top. When span A is above span B the cloud is usually green (bullish), and when span B is on top it is red (bearish). The cloud's thickness matters too: a thick cloud is a broad area of projected support or resistance, while a thin or twisted cloud is weaker and easier to break.

Because it is shifted forward, the cloud is really the chart's best guess at where price may find support or resistance in the bars to come — which is why Ichimoku traders read the current cloud under price as future support.

4. Price vs the cloud

The simplest Ichimoku read is position relative to the cloud:

  • Price above the cloud — the trend is considered bullish; the cloud below acts as support.
  • Price below the cloud — the trend is considered bearish; the cloud above acts as resistance.
  • Price inside the cloud — the picture is mixed; the market is often balancing or transitioning.
↑above the cloud — bullish bias ↓below the cloud — bearish bias ↔inside the cloud — mixed / balancing Kumothick cloud = stronger projected support

5. Chikou span confirmation

The Chikou span is today's closing price drawn 26 periods in the past. This backward shift lets you compare the current price to where the market was a while ago — if the Chikou sits above the candles from 26 bars back, momentum is confirming an uptrend; if it sits below, it confirms the opposite.

Traders use the Chikou as a confirmation filter. A bullish setup is stronger when the Chikou is also clear of past price, because two independent parts of the system agree. When the Chikou is tangled in the old candles, the signal is weaker.

6. A quick read

All five lines can be summarised as a short checklist. When they agree, the picture is clean; when they disagree, it is a warning to wait.

7. Common mistakes

  • Trading the Tenkan / Kijun cross alone. Wait for price position and the Chikou to agree before trusting a flip.
  • Reading the cloud as a wall. Price can and does pass through the Kumo; a thick cloud is an area, not a barrier.
  • Ignoring the forward shift. The cloud you see now is projected ahead — it can change as new bars arrive.
  • Using Ichimoku on tiny timeframes. The overlay was designed with longer horizons in mind; low timeframes produce a lot of noise.
  • Expecting one glance to replace a plan. Even a full system needs an entry, a stop and sensible risk.

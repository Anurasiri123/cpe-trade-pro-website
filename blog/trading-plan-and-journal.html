Build a Trading Plan and Journal That Actually Works

A plan turns trading from a series of moods into a series of decisions. Here is what to write down — markets, sessions, setup, entry, stop, targets, risk per trade and a daily loss cap — and how a journal makes review honest.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Almost every trader has an idea about how they trade, but very few write it down. That gap matters: a plan in your head quietly changes with your mood, getting looser after a win and stricter after a loss. A written plan does not.

This guide is about the unglamorous machinery of trading: the plan, the rules, the checklist, the journal and the review. None of it predicts price, but all of it decides whether your results come from a method or from luck. Educational content only — not financial advice.

Why you need a written plan

A plan exists to move decisions away from the moment of pressure. When a candle is moving and money is on the line, judgement is at its worst — exactly when you are most likely to widen a stop or chase a spike. If the rules were written in a calm moment, the pressured version of you has nothing to invent.

It also makes review possible. If you never wrote down why you took a trade, you cannot tell later whether a loss came from a bad idea or a badly executed good one — two very different problems, and without a plan and journal you will never know which you have.

The components of a plan

A workable plan is short — one page is enough. Every line should remove a decision you would otherwise make on the fly. Start with these seven parts.

ComponentWhat it fixesExample of a rule
MarketsWhere you are allowed to tradeBTC/USDT and ETH/USDT perpetuals only
SessionsWhen you are allowed to tradeLondon open and first two hours of New York
SetupThe pattern that justifies a tradeTrend pull-back into a level, aligned with the higher timeframe
Entry, stop, targetsWhere you get in and outEntry on the break, stop behind the level, two targets
Risk per tradeHow much one idea can costA fixed small fraction of the account per trade
Max daily lossWhen to stop for the dayStop trading after the daily cap is hit — no exceptions
Max positionsHow much you can hold at onceNo more than two correlated positions open together
💡
Write the plan while nothing is at stake Draft it on a weekend, with no positions open. A plan written during a live trade is not a plan — it is a justification.

Rules and checklists

Rules are the plan in action, and a checklist is how you stop yourself skipping them. Before every entry, run the same short list and proceed only when every box is ticked. If one fails, there is no trade — not a smaller trade, not a "just this once" trade.

  • Direction agreed with the higher timeframe? Trading against the trend is a harder game.
  • Does the setup match the written definition? If you must stretch it, it is not the setup.
  • Is the stop where the idea is logically invalidated? Not merely where you feel comfortable.
  • Is the risk within your per-trade rule? Size follows stop distance, not confidence.
  • Are you inside your daily loss limit? If you have hit it, the day is over.

🛑 Daily loss cap A hard number that ends the session. It protects tomorrow's judgement.

What to record in a journal

A journal is not a scoreboard, it is a mirror. The profit or loss is the least interesting column — what you want is the reasoning, so patterns in your behaviour become visible over dozens of trades rather than one.

  • The basics: date, market, direction, timeframe.
  • The reason: a sentence or two on why you took it. If you cannot write one, you did not have one.
  • The numbers: entry, stop, targets and the risk you accepted.
  • The state of mind: calm, bored, impatient, revengeful. Be blunt; nobody else reads this.
  • The outcome and the grade: the result, plus a mark for whether you followed the plan — a good process can still lose, and a bad one can still win.

That last point is the one most traders miss. A winning trade taken outside the plan is a warning, not a triumph, because it rewards the wrong behaviour. Grading execution separately from the result trains the process rather than the outcome.

Reviewing and backtesting

A journal that is never read is just a diary. Set a fixed review rhythm — weekly is a good default — and look for repeats, not individual wins. Are the losses clustered in one session? Do they follow a particular emotion? Are the rule-breaks rising or falling?

Backtesting is the same idea run over history instead of memory. Pick your setup, step through old charts, and record what the plan would have done — entry, stop, target, outcome — without hindsight. It will not predict the future, but it shows whether your rules are testable and whether you can follow them.

⚠️
A backtest is not a track record It is easy to curve-fit rules to the past. Treat backtesting as a way to check your discipline and your logic, never as evidence of future returns.

Order Blocks: A Complete Strategy Guide

The last candle before the move — and why traders watch it so closely. Here is what an order block is, how CPE Trade Pro draws them automatically, and how to trade the retest.

11 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Order blocks are one of the most talked-about ideas in modern chart reading, and one of the most misunderstood. They are not magic rectangles that price must respect. They are a way of marking where a move likely began — the last candle in the opposite direction before a strong push — so you have a logical place to look for a reaction.

This guide covers what an order block is, bullish versus bearish zones, how CPE Trade Pro detects and draws them, how to trade the retest, where to place your stop and targets, how to combine order blocks with fair value gaps and structure, and an honest look at when they fail. Educational content only — nothing here is financial advice.

What an order block is

An order block is the last candle (or small cluster of candles) in the opposite direction before an impulsive move that breaks structure. The idea is simple: for price to break out with force, someone had to be buying or selling size. The candle that immediately preceded that push is treated as the likely origin of those orders — an area where demand or supply may still sit.

A bullish order block is the last down candle before a strong up move; it is watched as a potential demand zone. A bearish order block is the last up candle before a strong down move; it is watched as potential supply. In both cases what makes the zone worth marking is the displacement that followed it.

ℹ️
Displacement is the point A quiet candle with no follow-through is just a candle. An order block earns its name from the impulsive move it precedes — the volatility is the evidence.

Bullish vs bearish order blocks

The two are mirror images, and keeping them straight is most of the work. The table below is the quick reference.

Formed byLast down candle before a strong up moveLast up candle before a strong down move
Treated asDemand — a place to look for longsSupply — a place to look for shorts
Invalidated byPrice closing clearly below the zonePrice closing clearly above the zone

The retest entry

The most common way to use an order block is not to chase the breakout but to wait for price to come back. If an up move left a bullish order block behind, the idea is to buy on the retest — when price returns to that zone — rather than at the top of the impulse. The retest gives you a defined area, a nearby invalidation point and a better distance to your target.

💡
Wait for the return, not the breakout Chasing the impulse means a wide stop and poor reward. Letting price come back to the zone usually gives a tighter invalidation and a more sensible risk-to-reward.

Stop and target placement

  • Stop loss — beyond the far edge of the zone, where the block is genuinely invalidated. If you are long from a bullish block, the stop goes below the low of the zone; if you are short from a bearish block, it goes above the high.
  • Target 1 — the first meaningful level in front of price: the prior swing high, an opposing order block, or a nearby liquidity zone.
  • Target 2 — the further objective, such as an untested higher-timeframe level or an extension.
  • Minimum gap — CPE Trade Pro enforces a minimum distance between entry and stop so the stop is never unrealistically tight for the market's volatility.

Before you enter, measure the distance from entry to stop and from entry to targets. If the reward does not justify the risk on your plan, the trade is simply not taken.

Order blocks + fair value gaps + BOS

An order block on its own is a decent reference; an order block that lines up with two other pieces of evidence is far stronger. The classic confluence trio is a break of structure that confirms the trend, a fair value gap showing the imbalance left by the move, and the order block at the origin. When all three sit at roughly the same price, the zone earns more attention.

When order blocks fail

Zones are probabilities, not promises, and they fail in ways you can learn to recognise.

  • Already mitigated. If price has tapped the zone once and reacted, the orders may be spent. A second or third tap is often weaker.
  • No displacement. A "block" with no impulsive move behind it was never really an order block.
  • Against higher-timeframe bias. A bullish block in a strongly bearish higher-timeframe chart is swimming upstream.
  • Through-trade. Sometimes price runs straight through the zone without pausing, especially into news.
  • Liquidity sweep first. Price may spike beyond the zone to trigger stops before reversing — which is why the stop should sit beyond the far edge, not inside it.
⚠️
Never trade a zone without a stop An order block is a location, not an excuse to hold. The invalidation point is what turns a zone into a managed trade — use it and do not widen it after entry.

A step-by-step checklist

  1. Check the higher-timeframe bias first. Prefer zones that agree with it.
  2. Confirm displacement and structure. There should be a real break, not a slow drift.
  3. Mark the zone — or let CPE Trade Pro draw the order block, FVG and BOS for you.
  4. List the confluence. Does the zone overlap a fair value gap or liquidity level?
  5. Wait for the retest. Do not chase the impulse; let price return to the zone.
  6. Place the stop beyond the zone's far edge and check the app's minimum-gap level.
  7. Set targets at the next logical levels — target 1 nearer, target 2 further.
  8. Size the position to the risk and skip the trade if reward does not justify it.

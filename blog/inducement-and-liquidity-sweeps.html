Inducement (IDM) Explained: Why Price Traps You Before It Moves

You enter on a textbook setup and it reverses against you seconds later. Often it is not bad luck — it is inducement. Here is how the trap is set, and how to stop falling for it.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

If you have traded for any length of time, you know the feeling. You spot an obvious support, buy the bounce, and price knocks out your stop by a few ticks before finally doing exactly what you expected. It looks personal. It is not — it is structural. Price needs liquidity to move, and the most reliable pool of liquidity is the crowd's stop orders sitting just beyond an obvious level.

Smart-money concepts give that behaviour a name: inducement, often shortened to IDM. This guide explains what inducement is, how it connects to liquidity sweeps and stop hunts, why equal highs and lows are the classic bait, and how to fold it into a disciplined reading of the chart. What inducement is

Inducement is a deliberately placed, easy-to-see swing that sits before the real area of interest. It exists to tempt impatient traders into acting early. Picture a big liquidity zone far below the current price. Between you and that zone, there is one clean little low that practically shouts "buy here". Traders buy, their stops stack just under it, and that tidy pool of orders becomes the fuel price needs to drive down into the real zone.

The trap works because it exploits a natural human instinct — the urge to trade the first opportunity you see instead of waiting for the one the market has been building toward. Inducement is not random noise. It is temptation placed on purpose, and once you can name it, you start to see it everywhere.

Liquidity sweeps and stop hunts

Inducement is the bait; a liquidity sweep is the act of taking it. When price drives through an obvious high or low, it triggers the stop orders resting there and fills any pending orders, giving the market the counterparty volume it needs to reverse and continue in the intended direction. Traders often call this a stop hunt, because from their side it feels like the market hunted their stop.

The tell of a genuine sweep is speed and rejection. Price pokes through the level sharply, then snaps back and closes on the original side within a candle or two. A level that is simply broken — with price continuing and holding beyond it — is not a sweep; it is a real break. Confusing the two is expensive, which is why the way price behaves after the poke matters more than the poke itself.

Equal highs and lows as bait

The most visible bait of all is a pair of equal highs or equal lows. Two or more matched extremes look like a wall — resistance that price "cannot" break, or support that "must" hold. Traders place orders and stops around them, which is precisely why they attract a sweep.

Double tops and triple bottoms are the same story told with patterns. The second or third touch looks like a confirmation, but it is often just a fresh pool of trapped orders for the market to consume. When price finally pierces those equal lows, it is not a breakdown — very often it is the sweep that precedes a rally. Reading it that way flips a confusing move into a legible one.

How to avoid being trapped

You cannot eliminate being swept — but you can stop feeding the pool. The first habit is patience: do not take the first obvious level you see, especially when a larger, more significant level sits just behind it. The second is placement: keep your invalidation beyond the liquidity, not in front of it, so a routine sweep does not remove you.

  • Wait for the sweep. Let price take the obvious low or high first, then look for your entry on the reaction.
  • Stop under the pool, not above it. Give your stop room beyond where the crowd's stops sit.
  • Prefer the higher-timeframe level. If two levels compete, the bigger one usually wins.
  • Demand confirmation. A structure break or a clean rejection after the sweep is stronger than a guess before it.
⚠️
IDM is a probability, not a certainty Not every obvious level is inducement, and not every sweep reverses. Treat these reads as improved odds, never as guarantees — sizing and risk control are what protect you when the read is wrong.

Combining IDM with order blocks and FVGs

Inducement is most useful as part of a chain, not on its own. In practice you look for a significant order block or fair value gap — the area you actually want to trade from — then check whether there is an inducement sitting in front of it. If there is, you have a reason to wait: price may sweep the IDM, then run into your zone, giving a cleaner entry with a tighter invalidation.

The sequence is what makes it work: identify the real zone, spot the bait in front of it, wait for the sweep, then act on the reaction into the zone. Treated this way, inducement stops being a hazard and becomes a timing filter — it tells you when the market has finished gathering the liquidity it needs to move, which is often the best moment to be a buyer or seller with the crowd that got trapped.

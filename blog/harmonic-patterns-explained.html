Harmonic Patterns Explained: Gartley, Bat, Butterfly, Crab, Shark and 5-0

Harmonic patterns turn Fibonacci ratios into a shape. Here is the XABCD structure, the ratios behind the classic patterns, and how to trade the PRZ — honestly, with no promise of a magic edge.

12 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Most chart patterns are described by eye: "it looks like a head and shoulders", "it looks like a triangle". Harmonic patterns are different. They are defined by numbers. Every swing in the formation has to land on a specific Fibonacci ratio of the leg before it, and if the numbers do not fit, the pattern simply is not there. That precision is the whole appeal — and the whole difficulty.

This guide explains what harmonic patterns are, the five-point XABCD framework they share, the ratios behind the main names (Gartley, Bat, Butterfly, Crab, Shark and 5-0), and how traders use the potential reversal zone for entries, stops and targets. It is educational content only — nothing here is financial advice, and no pattern can promise a profit.

What harmonic patterns are

A harmonic pattern is a five-point geometric formation drawn across a series of price swings. Instead of eyeballing a shape, you measure each leg as a ratio of the one before it and check that every measurement falls within a known band. When all five points line up with the numbers, the pattern is "valid"; when even one ratio is out of range, it is not.

The idea came from H.M. Gartley, whose 1935 book described a specific reversal shape, and was later expanded by traders such as Larry Pesavento and Scott Carney, who formalised the ratios and named the additional variants. The theory rests on one assumption: that markets reverse at predictable Fibonacci relationships, because traders naturally watch the same levels and react at the same time. That assumption is worth holding loosely — it is useful, not certain.

Because the pattern is so strictly defined, it is a natural fit for software. A program can measure the ratios in milliseconds and tell you whether a candidate formation qualifies, which is exactly the kind of thing a manual analyst finds tedious. The XABCD structure and Fibonacci ratios

Almost every harmonic pattern is built from the same five points, labelled X, A, B, C and D. Working through a bullish example: X is the starting low, A is the first high, B is the pull-back, C is the bounce, and D is the final low where the pattern completes and a trader looks to buy. A bearish pattern is simply the mirror image.

Three ratios do most of the work. The AB leg is measured as a retracement of XA. The BC leg is measured as a retracement of AB. The CD leg — and the all-important D point — is measured as an extension of BC and, crucially, as a retracement or extension of the original XA leg. The D point is the trigger: it is where the pattern is finished and where the trade idea lives.

The main patterns and their ratios

The named patterns differ in where D lands relative to X. Some complete inside the XA range (a retracement); others overshoot X (an extension). That single difference changes how deep the pattern goes, how tight the stop can be, and how aggressive the reversal may be.

The PRZ and entry, stop and target

The PRZ — Potential Reversal Zone — is the small area where several Fibonacci measurements overlap at once. For a bullish Gartley, that cluster might sit at the 0.786 retracement of XA, the 1.618 extension of BC and a projected level from CD, all within a few points of each other. The more ratios that agree in one spot, the more meaningful the zone tends to be.

Trading the zone is mechanical. You wait for price to enter the PRZ, then look for the formation to complete, and place the entry inside the zone. The stop goes just beyond the far edge — past the X point for a retracement pattern, or past the D extreme for an extension pattern. The targets are usually the prior swing highs and lows: the 0.382 and 0.618 retracements of the CD leg are common first objectives, with the full CD measured as a further goal.

💡
Treat the PRZ as an area, not a price No pattern fires at a single exact tick. Give the zone a little room, wait for a reaction candle to form inside it, and let the market confirm before you commit.

Confirmation and common mistakes

Harmonic patterns look beautiful in hindsight and slippery in real time. The ratios are exact, but the swings you feed into them are a judgement call, so it is easy to "find" a pattern where none truly exists. A few honest guardrails help.

  • Do not force the fit. If the AB or BC ratio is outside the band, it is not that pattern. Wait for one that qualifies instead of bending the numbers.
  • Always find the cluster. A single Fibonacci level is not a PRZ. Look for two or three measurements landing together before you take the idea seriously.
  • Wait for the completion. A pattern is not live until price actually reaches D. Anticipating the turn early is where most harmonic losses come from.
  • Confirm with structure. A reversal candle, a break of the prior short-term swing, or a shift in momentum all add weight. The pattern tells you where; confirmation tells you when. See chart patterns and how CPE Trade Pro analyses a chart for that structural read.
  • Respect the stop. The invalidation level is the whole point. If price closes beyond D, the pattern failed and the trade is over.
⚠️
A valid pattern is still just a possibility Clear ratios and a tidy PRZ do not raise your odds of profit to anything close to certain. Size positions so that a normal losing trade is genuinely survivable, and never let a "perfect" pattern talk you past your risk limit.

Spot vs futures crypto trading: what is the difference?

Two markets, two very different risk profiles. Here is what spot trading and futures trading actually mean, how leverage, funding rates and liquidation work — and why CPE Trade Pro treats Spot as long-only and routes short setups to Futures.

8 min read Updated 2026 Category Markets

When people say "I trade crypto," they usually mean one of two things. Either they bought an asset and now own it, or they are trading a contract whose value follows that asset. The first is spot. The second is futures. The difference sounds small, but it changes almost everything about how you plan, size and manage a trade.

1. What spot trading is

In spot trading you buy and sell the actual asset. When you buy one coin on spot, you own one coin. It sits in your account until you decide to sell it, send it, or hold it. There is no expiry date and no contract to roll over.

Because you simply own something, spot is naturally long-only: you buy first, then you sell higher. You cannot "go short" on spot in the straightforward sense, because going short would mean selling an asset you do not actually hold. So spot is the simplest, most intuitive way to trade — the same idea as buying gold at a shop.

  • You own the asset. It is yours, not a claim on something.
  • Direction is up. Spot profits when price rises and is closed to stop losses when price falls.
  • No leverage by default. You risk what you put in.
  • No funding and no liquidation on a plain spot position.
💡
Short version Spot = you own the coin, long-only, no expiry. Futures = you trade a contract, can go long or short, usually with leverage — which also brings funding and liquidation.

2. What futures and perpetual contracts are

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price. In crypto, the most popular form is the perpetual contract — a futures contract with no expiry date that simply keeps tracking the spot price. Most exchanges offer USDT-margined perpetuals, where profit and loss are settled in USDT.

Two features make futures different from spot:

  • You can go long or short. A long profits when price rises; a short profits when price falls. This is how you take a bearish view.
  • You can use leverage. You post a smaller amount of margin to control a larger position. Leverage magnifies both gains and losses.

Because leverage exists, futures introduces two things spot never has: funding rates and liquidation.

3. Funding rates and liquidation, in simple terms

A funding rate is a small payment exchanged between long and short traders on a perpetual contract, usually every few hours. When the rate is positive, longs pay shorts; when it is negative, shorts pay longs. It exists to keep the perpetual price close to the spot price. For a trader holding a position for a while, funding is a small ongoing cost (or tiny credit), not a fee you pay once.

Liquidation is the bigger idea. With leverage, your margin is your cushion. If price moves far enough against your position that the cushion runs out, the exchange force-closes the trade. That is liquidation. The higher the leverage, the smaller the move needed to hit it.

⚠️
Leverage magnifies losses A 10x position can be wiped out by a move of roughly 10% against it. Leverage does not make a bad idea good — it just makes the result arrive faster, in both directions.

5. Risk differences

Spot and futures carry very different risk even on the same asset and the same chart.

  • Spot risk is limited to what you own. If price falls, your position loses value, but nothing forces you out. You can hold and wait.
  • Futures risk includes liquidation. With leverage, a bad move can close your position for you at the worst time, before the market recovers.
  • Futures risk includes funding. Held long enough, funding quietly eats into the trade.
  • Shorting risk is theoretically unbounded. A short loses more as price rises, and price can rise without a known ceiling.

7. Which one suits a beginner?

If you are new, spot is the better place to start. There is no liquidation to fear, no funding to track, and the mechanics match the simple idea of "buy low, sell high." You can learn how signals, entries, stops and targets behave without leverage turning a small mistake into a large one.

Move to futures only once you understand leverage, margin and liquidation properly — and only with size you can afford to lose. A sensible path is: learn on spot, practise on the app's DEMO mode, then try futures small. The app's DEMO paper trading is designed exactly for that middle step.

8. Summary

Spot means owning the asset: simple, long-only, no liquidation. Futures means trading a contract: you can go long or short, usually with leverage, and that brings funding rates and the risk of liquidation. Both are useful. The mistake is treating them as the same thing, or using leverage before you understand it.

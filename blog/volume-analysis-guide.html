Volume explained: reading participation behind a move

Price tells you what happened. Volume tells you how many traders were involved — and that is often the difference between a move worth trusting and one that fades the moment you enter.

8 min read Updated 2026 Category Indicators

Every candle has a fifth number beyond open, high, low and close: volume. It is easy to ignore, because it sits in a small strip at the bottom of the chart. That would be a mistake. Volume is the closest thing a chart has to a headcount — it shows how many participants were prepared to act at that price.

This guide covers what volume really measures, how to compare a bar against its average, why breakouts live or die on volume, and the two volume studies worth knowing. It ends with how CPE Trade Pro folds volume into its analysis.

1. What volume really measures

Volume is the quantity traded during a period — the number of coins, shares or contracts that changed hands. It does not say who was right. It says how much activity took place.

That distinction matters. A huge candle on huge volume means a lot of traders agreed to transact at those prices. A huge candle on tiny volume means price moved, but almost nobody traded. The first move has conviction behind it; the second can be a single large order slipping through a thin book.

Volume is best read relative to itself. Absolute numbers vary wildly between markets and between a quiet weekend and a busy news day. What you actually compare is today's bar against the recent average — and that is where the volume average line comes in.

2. Volume bars and the average line

Under each candle sits a vertical bar whose height is the traded volume for that bar. Colour usually matches the candle — green for an up bar, red for a down bar. Drawn across the top of those bars is a moving average of volume, typically over 20 periods. That average is your baseline for "normal".

  • A bar near the average — ordinary activity, nothing special to read.
  • A bar well above the average — a volume spike. Something made traders act: news, a level breaking, or a large position being worked.
  • A bar well below the average — thin participation. Moves on thin volume are easier to reverse.

The average line is what turns a raw number into a signal. One million in volume means nothing until you know the recent average was four hundred thousand.

💡
Compare, don't count Raw volume is a number without context. What matters is the ratio to the recent average. A "spike" is simply a bar that stands out against the line, not a fixed quantity.

3. Breakouts with and without volume

The clearest use of volume is on breakouts — the moment price leaves a range or clears a level. Two breakouts can look identical on the price chart and behave completely differently, depending on the volume behind them.

A breakout with a volume spike shows that many traders acted as price cleared the level. The move has participation behind it, which tends to make it more likely to hold. A breakout on thin volume shows price slipped through without a crowd following. Those are the ones that often snap back — a false break.

That is why volume works best as a confirmation filter. The price chart proposes the breakout; volume votes on whether it is real.

4. MFI and OBV in one line

Two volume-based studies add a second layer beyond the raw bars.

The Money Flow Index (MFI) is often called "volume-weighted RSI". It takes each bar's typical price and its volume, then asks whether money flowed in or out. It returns a 0-100 reading: high values suggest buying pressure, low values selling pressure. Because it includes volume, many traders treat it as a pressure gauge that a price-only oscillator cannot see.

On-Balance Volume (OBV) is a running total. It adds volume on up bars and subtracts volume on down bars, producing a single line. The line's direction matters more than its level: a rising OBV says up-bars are carrying more volume than down-bars, a falling OBV says the opposite.

Both are best read for divergence and direction, not for exact timing. If price makes a new high and OBV does not, participation is not confirming the high — worth knowing, but not a signal by itself.

5. Volume red flags

A handful of patterns are worth circling whenever they appear.

What you seeWhy it is a caution flag
A big move on very thin volumeFew participants; the move can reverse quickly when normal volume returns
A breakout with no volume spikeNo crowd behind the break — a common false break
A huge spike at the end of a long trendCan mark exhaustion or a large position being absorbed
Price rising while OBV fallsUp-bars are carrying less volume than down-bars — participation is fading
Spikes that appear only at round numbersOften stop and option activity rather than genuine conviction
✅ConfirmsBreakout plus a volume spike — participation agrees with price.
⚠️WarnsBig move, thin volume — the crowd is not there.
📉DivergesPrice up, OBV down — participation is quietly fading.
🔎ContextCompare every bar to the average, never in isolation.

6. Common mistakes

  • Reading volume without an average. A number alone says nothing; the ratio to the average is the whole point.
  • Assuming high volume means higher price. Volume marks activity, not direction.
  • Chasing a spike blindly. A spike can confirm a move or cap it — context decides which.
  • Trading thin markets. Low-volume pairs move on small orders and are hard to exit at a fair price.
  • Ignoring divergence. Price and OBV pointing different ways is information, not noise.

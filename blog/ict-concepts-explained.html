ICT Concepts Explained: Liquidity, Killzones and the Inner Circle Trader Model

ICT is one of the most popular — and most misunderstood — approaches in modern trading. Here is the honest version: liquidity, killzones, optimal trade entry, fair value gaps and order blocks, in plain English.

13 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

ICT stands for Inner Circle Trader, a body of market-structure ideas developed and taught by Michael J. Huddleston. At its core it offers one explanation for why price moves the way it does: markets travel between pools of resting orders, and the biggest players are looking for the liquidity those orders represent. Learn where the orders sit, and you can guess where price is headed next.

That is the theory, and it is appealing because it turns a messy chart into a story. But it is a story, not a proven law. This guide explains the main ICT building blocks honestly — what liquidity pools are, how killzones time the day, what optimal trade entry, fair value gaps and order blocks mean, and how Power of 3 and the Judas swing fit in. Educational content only; nothing here is financial advice.

What ICT (Inner Circle Trader) is

ICT is less a single indicator than a way of reading a chart. Instead of asking "which pattern is this?", it asks three questions: where is the liquidity?, what time of day is it?, and what imbalance has price left behind? Everything else — order blocks, fair value gaps, optimal trade entry — is a tool for answering those three questions.

The central claim is that price is drawn toward liquidity. Old highs, old lows, equal highs and equal lows all hold clusters of stop orders, and the market is assumed to reach for those clusters before continuing. Whether you believe a shadowy "smart money" is doing the reaching or simply that markets naturally sweep obvious levels, the practical takeaway is the same: obvious levels attract price.

Liquidity pools and stops

A liquidity pool is a cluster of resting orders sitting at a level the market can see. Above old highs sit buy stops — short sellers' exits plus breakout buyers' entries. Below old lows sit sell stops — long traders' exits plus breakdown sellers. ICT calls these buy-side and sell-side liquidity.

The ICT reading is that price often runs through these levels to "take" the liquidity before reversing. A spike above an obvious high that immediately fails is described as a liquidity grab or stop hunt. It is a useful lens, but be careful: not every wick above a high is a stop hunt. Sometimes price simply keeps going. If you want the deeper structural background, our guide to Smart Money Concepts covers the same ground.

Killzones, Power of 3 and the Judas swing

ICT is as much about time as price. Sessions behave differently, so the model divides the day into killzones — windows where activity and volatility tend to concentrate. The exact clock times vary by broker and season, but the shape of the day is consistent.

Two more ICT ideas live on this same timeline. Power of 3 describes the typical day as three acts: accumulation (a quiet range), manipulation (a false move that traps early traders), then distribution (the real directional expansion). The false move in the middle is the Judas swing — the deceptive push that runs one way before price reverses and delivers the day's actual direction.

The point of naming these phases is behavioural, not mystical. Traders who jump in on the first move of a session are frequently the ones whose stops fuel the reversal. ICT uses that tendency as a filter: wait for the manipulation to play out, then trade the expansion.

AccumulationA tight range early in the sessionOrders building on both sides before a move
ManipulationA sharp false break one wayThe Judas swing — a trap that takes out stops

💡
Trade the reaction, not the prediction The Judas swing only makes sense in hindsight. In real time, the useful discipline is to wait for the manipulation to fail — a failed break — before you commit to the expansion.

Optimal trade entry and the OTE zone

OTE stands for Optimal Trade Entry. It is the 62% to 79% retracement of a swing leg, taken in the direction of your higher-timeframe bias. Within that band, the 70.5% level is treated as the "sweet spot". In practice you mark a strong impulse leg, wait for price to retrace into that zone, and look to enter with the trend.

The idea is a familiar one dressed in new clothes: buying a pull-back in an uptrend rather than chasing the highs. What ICT adds is the insistence on confluence. An OTE entry is considered stronger when the 62–79% band also coincides with a fair value gap or an order block, so you are stacking several reasons to act in the same small area.

Fair value gaps and order blocks in ICT terms

ICT describes imbalance through two signed patterns. A fair value gap (FVG) is a three-candle formation where the move is so fast that a gap is left between the first and third candle's wicks — a stretch of the chart where price barely traded. ICT expects price to return and "rebalance" that gap before continuing. You can read the full mechanics in our guide to the fair value gap.

An order block is the last opposing candle before a strong directional move — for example, the final down candle before a big rally is a bullish order block. ICT treats that candle's range as a zone where institutional orders were supposedly placed. Again, the concept is a plausible narrative rather than a proof; what matters practically is that many traders watch these zones, which can make them self-reinforcing. Our guide to reading order blocks covers the detail.

🟦 Fair value gap A three-candle imbalance price often returns to fill before it continues.
🟪 Order block The last opposite candle before a strong move, treated as a reaction zone.
📍 Breaker A failed order block that price later tests from the other side.

How it relates to SMC

If much of this sounds familiar, it is because ICT and Smart Money Concepts share almost all of their vocabulary. SMC is the broader community label; ICT is the original body of work. Terms like liquidity, order blocks, fair value gaps, break of structure (BOS and CHoCH) and market structure shifts flow freely between the two.

The honest summary is this: ICT gives you a coherent way to think about where price reacts (liquidity levels and imbalances), when it tends to move (killzones and the daily cycle) and how a move can be quantified (OTE and structure). That is genuinely useful for building a repeatable plan. What it is not is a crystal ball — the "smart money" framing is a narrative, and a tidy ICT chart in hindsight is often much messier in real time.

⚠️
Watch the over-explaining ICT's richness is also its trap: with so many concepts, it is easy to find a reason for any outcome. If a trade only makes sense after you have added three more concepts, it was probably not a good trade to begin with.

ADX and DMI explained: how strong is the trend?

The ADX and the +DI / −DI lines answer two different questions — how strong is the trend and which side is winning. Read them together and you stop confusing a busy chart with a tradeable one.

8 min read Updated 2026 Category Indicators

Ask two traders what ADX means and you may get two answers: "trend strength" and "a buy signal". Only the first one is right. The Average Directional Index, and the Directional Movement Indicators that sit underneath it, describe how strong a trend is and which side is pushing. On their own they do not tell you when to buy or sell.

This guide covers what each line means, the well-known 25 threshold, the four market states the indicator can fall into, and how the whole thing feeds into CPE Trade Pro's analysis.

1. Trend strength vs direction

Most tools answer one of two questions: which way or how much. Moving averages and the directional lines lean toward direction. Oscillators like RSI lean toward momentum. ADX answers a third question that traders often skip — is there a trend at all, and how strong is it?

Strength and direction are different things, and mixing them up is the single most common way ADX is misused. A strong rally and a strong sell-off both push ADX higher, because it measures the size of the directional push, not its sign. That is why ADX is called a non-directional index.

So you read the two together. ADX tells you whether a trend is worth trading at all; the +DI and −DI lines tell you which direction it points. One without the other is half a picture.

2. The ADX line and the 25 rule

ADX is a single line, usually running between 0 and 100. It is built from how consistently price moves in one direction relative to its recent range, then smoothed. The number itself matters less than its level and its slope — how high it is and whether it is rising or falling.

Some widely used reference points:

  • Below 20 — little or no trend; the market is drifting or ranging.
  • 20 to 25 — a trend may be starting; watch for the slope to turn up.
  • Above 25 — the market is generally considered to be trending.
  • Above 40 — a strong trend. Above 50 — very strong, and often late in its run.

A rising ADX means the trend is strengthening. A falling ADX means it is weakening, even if price keeps drifting the same way for a while. That distinction — rising versus falling — usually matters more than the exact reading.

The 25 rule A common way to filter is simple: only trust trend-following ideas when ADX is above 25 and rising. Below 20, most trend tools hand you false starts, because there is no trend to follow.

3. +DI and −DI

Underneath the ADX sit two lines: the Positive Directional Indicator (+DI) and the Negative Directional Indicator (−DI). They measure how much of the recent directional movement was upward (+DI) versus downward (−DI).

When +DI is above −DI, upward movement dominates. When −DI is above +DI, downward movement dominates. A cross between the two is a shift in who is stronger — from sellers to buyers, or the reverse.

The important detail: a cross on its own is weak. In a ranging market the two lines cross constantly and give nothing but noise. A cross becomes far more meaningful when the ADX is rising at the same time, because that confirms a genuine trend is forming rather than a back-and-forth drift.

⚠️
A cross alone is not a signal In a choppy market the two lines cross again and again. Wait for the ADX to rise through 25 to confirm the cross actually means something. Direction without strength is a coin flip.

Putting the two readings together gives you four broad states, each with its own sensible approach.

5. Settings

The default length of 14 periods works well for most charts. Shortening it makes ADX react faster but noisier; lengthening it smooths the line and delays it. A few practical choices:

It is also worth noting that ADX is often a lagging reading. By the time it is comfortably above 40, a move may be mature. Many traders use it as a permission filter rather than a trigger: it says whether the conditions suit a trend trade, not the exact moment to enter.

6. Common mistakes

  • Treating ADX as directional. A high ADX says a trend exists, not which way it points. The DI lines settle that.
  • Trading every DI cross. In a range the lines cross non-stop. Require the ADX to confirm.
  • Chasing the extreme. ADX above 50 can mean a move is strong — and stretched. Strength is not a promise it continues.
  • Ignoring the slope. A falling ADX inside an uptrend warns the trend is losing energy, even while price edges higher.
  • Using ADX alone. It says nothing about where to enter, where to exit, or where the idea is wrong.

Bollinger Bands Explained: Volatility, the Squeeze and Band Walk

Bollinger Bands wrap price in a moving envelope that stretches and shrinks with volatility. Here is what the three lines are, what the squeeze and band walk really tell you, and how CPE Trade Pro reads band edges as levels.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Bollinger Bands are one of the few indicators that say something price alone cannot: how wide the market's normal range is right now. John Bollinger built them to be an envelope around price, not a signal generator, and the traders who get the most from them treat the bands as context rather than commands.

What the bands show

A Bollinger Band set is three lines around price. The middle line is a simple moving average. The two outer lines sit a fixed number of standard deviations above and below it. Because standard deviation measures how spread out recent prices are, the envelope widens when the market is volatile and narrows when it is calm.

That is the whole idea: the bands are a live picture of volatility. When they stretch, moves are large; when they pinch together, the market has gone quiet. They say nothing about direction on their own — an envelope can expand to either side.

The middle, upper and lower band (figure)

The default construction is a 20-period moving average with bands 2 standard deviations away. Roughly speaking, price spends most of its time inside the envelope, which is what makes the outer lines useful as reference levels.

The squeeze and the breakout (figure)

When volatility falls, the bands contract until they are unusually close together. Traders call this a squeeze. It means the market has coiled — the range has become so tight that a larger move is likely to follow. What a squeeze does not tell you is direction. It is a coiled spring, and the spring can release either way.

ℹ️
Squeeze = timing, not direction A squeeze tells you when to pay attention, not which way to trade. Combine it with structure or a band break to get a direction, and never trade the squeeze alone.

Walking the band

The counter-intuitive part of Bollinger Bands: price tagging the upper band is not automatically a sell. In a strong trend, price can walk the upper band for many bars, closing near it again and again while the middle line catches up underneath. This is a sign of strength, not exhaustion.

The same happens on the way down — price can walk the lower band. A useful habit is to watch the middle band as the trend's soft support (or resistance). As long as price keeps closing on the outside of the middle line, the walk tends to continue; a decisive close back through the middle is the first sign the walk is over.

Whether the move behind the walk has real participation is a good question to ask, and it is covered in our volume analysis guide.

Band width and volatility (table)

Band width is simply how far apart the outer lines are, and it moves in a rhythm. Reading that rhythm is often more useful than reacting to a single touch.

Settings (20, 2)

The default settings are worth keeping until you have a clear reason to change them:

  • Length 20. A middle-of-the-road average that reacts without being twitchy. Shorten it and price escapes the envelope constantly; lengthen it and the bands lag far behind.
  • Standard deviations 2. A multiplier. Use 2 for the standard view; wider multipliers (2.5, 3) keep price inside more, narrower ones push the bands in.
  • Band width basis. Some platforms let you plot bandwidth itself — a common way to spot squeezes objectively.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the bands as overbought/oversold. They are volatility boundaries; touching one is not a reversal signal.
  • Shorting every upper-band tag. In a trend, walking the band is strength — shorting into it is fighting the move.
  • Trading a squeeze without direction. The squeeze tells you a move is near, not which way.
  • Ignoring the width. Reading a single touch while ignoring whether the envelope is stretched is half the picture.
  • Using bands alone. Bands describe volatility; structure and momentum describe direction. Use them together.

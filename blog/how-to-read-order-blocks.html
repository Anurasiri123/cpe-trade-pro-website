How to read order blocks on a chart

An order block is one of the most useful zones in Smart Money Concepts. This is a step-by-step way to find one, mark it correctly, and plan an entry, stop and target around it — even if you have never drawn a zone by hand.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Technical analysis

If you have ever watched price shoot up from a level and then come back to that exact spot later, you have already seen an order block in action. In this guide we will look at what an order block is, how to tell a bullish one from a bearish one, how to mark the zone on your chart, and how to build a trade plan around it.

💡
Short version An order block is the last opposite candle before a strong move that breaks structure. You mark the zone, wait for price to return, and plan an entry with a stop beyond the zone and a target at the next level.

1. What is an order block?

An order block (OB) is the last opposite-coloured candle — or a small cluster of candles — immediately before an impulsive move that breaks market structure. The idea is that a large order was likely filled in that area, which is why price reacted so strongly from it.

An order block is not just any big candle. It only matters when the move that follows it is a real displacement: a fast, wide move that takes out a recent swing high or swing low. That displacement is the evidence that meaningful size was traded there.

Think of an order block as a memory of a battle. Buyers and sellers met there once, one side won, and price left quickly. Zones like that often get revisited, because unfilled orders may still be resting in that area.

2. Bullish vs bearish order blocks

There are two directions, and they mirror each other:

  • Bullish order block — the last down (bearish) candle before a strong up move that breaks structure. It usually acts as a demand zone.
  • Bearish order block — the last up (bullish) candle before a strong down move that breaks structure. It usually acts as a supply zone.

The two are not interchangeable. A bullish order block is only meaningful if the higher-timeframe trend backs it up — a lone demand zone inside a strong downtrend is a low-quality idea.

3. How to identify an order block (step by step)

Here is a simple, repeatable process you can apply on any chart:

  1. Find a displacement move. Look for a fast, wide candle or run of candles that stands out from the surrounding price action.
  2. Check that it broke structure. The move should close beyond the most recent swing high (for a bullish OB) or swing low (for a bearish OB).
  3. Step back to the last opposite candle. Before that move began, find the final candle that pointed the other way. That is your order block.
  4. Confirm it is unbroken. If price has already closed back through the candle, the block is spent — move on.
  5. Draw the zone. Box the candle (or the small cluster) and extend the box to the right so it is ready for a future retest.
  6. Wait for the return. Do not buy or sell the breakout. Wait for price to come back into the zone before you look for an entry.
  7. Plan the trade. Set the entry inside the zone, the stop beyond it, and targets at the next structural level.
⚠️
Displacement is the filter If the candle did not lead to a move that broke structure, it is not an order block — it is just a candle. Never mark a zone that has no displacement behind it.

4. How to mark the zone: body or wick?

Once you have your candle, you have to decide how tall the box should be. There are two common approaches:

  • Body only — box just the open-to-close range of the candle. Cleaner and stricter, and it produces a smaller zone that requires price to come deeper before you act.
  • Full candle (body plus wicks) — box the high-to-low range. Wider, so price touches it more easily, but that can lead to earlier and lower-quality fills.

A practical middle ground many traders use: mark the body first, and treat the wick as a secondary area of interest. If price only wicks into the candle and closes back out strongly, that can still be a reaction — but the body is where the main decision sits. The important thing is to be consistent: whichever method you pick, apply it the same way every time so your zones are comparable.

5. Broken vs unbroken order blocks

An order block is fresh (unbroken) until price closes through it. Once a candle closes beyond the far side of the zone, the block is considered broken or mitigated — the orders there have likely been filled, and the zone loses its meaning.

  • Unbroken: price has not yet traded back into or through the zone. The cleanest type to work with.
  • Touched but held: price tested the zone and respected it. Still valid, but watch for a second test.
  • Broken: price closed through the zone. Stop using it as a demand or supply area.

A simple filter is to only trade zones that are still intact and reasonably close to current price. A perfect order block that sits far away from the market is not actionable right now.

6. Entry, stop and target around an order block

With the zone marked, the trade plan almost writes itself:

  • Entry: inside the zone, ideally after a reaction candle confirms buyers or sellers are stepping in. Aggressive traders enter at the first touch; patient traders wait for a small structure shift in their favour inside the zone.
  • Stop: beyond the far edge of the zone. For a bullish OB, that is below the block; for a bearish OB, above it. If price closes cleanly through the zone, the idea is wrong.
  • Target: the next structural level — a prior swing high or low, or the opposite order block. Many traders scale out at two targets rather than closing the whole position at once.

Position size comes from the distance between entry and stop. If the stop is far away, the position should be smaller. Risking a fixed, small percentage of the account on each idea keeps a run of losses survivable.

7. Timeframe alignment

Order blocks work far better when the timeframes agree. A common approach:

  • Use a higher timeframe (for example the 4-hour or daily) to find the main trend and the big zones.
  • Use a lower timeframe (for example the 15-minute or 1-hour) to refine the entry inside that zone.

A bullish order block on the 5-minute chart means very little if the daily chart is crashing. Aligning direction on the higher timeframe and precision on the lower one removes a large share of bad trades.

8. Common mistakes

  • Marking zones with no displacement. No structure break means no order block.
  • Trading every zone. Most zones never get a clean reaction — filter by trend and distance.
  • Ignoring broken blocks. A closed-through zone is history, not a level.
  • Entering the breakout instead of the retest. The whole point of an order block is the return to the zone.
  • No stop. If you cannot define where the idea is wrong, you cannot manage the trade.

10. Summary

Reading order blocks is a simple skill once you have a routine: find the displacement, confirm the structure break, step back to the last opposite candle, mark the zone, check it is unbroken, then wait for the retest before planning entry, stop and target. Combine it with higher-timeframe direction and strict risk control, and order blocks become a practical part of your toolkit rather than a mystery box.

VWAP Explained: The Volume-Weighted Average Price

One line that tells you the average price everyone paid today, weighted by how much they traded. Here is how VWAP is built, why traders treat it as fair value, and how CPE Trade Pro reads it.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Most indicators are built from price alone. VWAP is different: it is built from price and volume together, which is why intraday traders watch it more closely than almost any other line on the chart. It answers a simple question — what is the average price paid for this instrument today, weighted by how much traded at each level?

This guide covers what VWAP is, how it is calculated, how to read price above and below it, anchored VWAP, and how CPE Trade Pro folds a VWAP series into its analysis. Educational content only — nothing here is financial advice.

What VWAP is

VWAP stands for Volume-Weighted Average Price. It is a single line that starts fresh at the beginning of a session and is recalculated on every new candle. Rather than averaging every candle equally, it gives more weight to the candles where the most volume traded.

That distinction matters. A price that only traded on thin volume barely moves the line, while a price where heavy volume changed hands pulls it strongly. The result is a line that reflects where the real business was done, not just where price happened to tick.

Because it resets each session, VWAP is an intraday tool by nature. It tells you about today, not about the last three months. That is the source of both its usefulness and its limits.

How it is calculated

The formula is not complicated. For each candle, take a representative price — usually the typical price, (high + low + close) / 3 — multiply it by that candle's volume, and accumulate. Then divide the running total by the running total volume:

VWAP = Σ(typical price × volume) ÷ Σ(volume)

Notice how candle 2, with three times the volume of candle 1, moves the average far more than an equal-sized price move on light volume would. The line keeps updating from the first candle to the last, so it is always anchored to the session's history.

VWAP as the day's fair value

Traders call VWAP a fair-value line because it is the break-even for everyone who traded today. If you bought below VWAP on average, you are in profit against the day's crowd; if you bought above it, you are behind the average. Institutions use it as a benchmark too — a desk instructed to buy "at or better than VWAP" is judged against exactly this line.

That benchmark status is why price so often reacts around VWAP. When the market is trying to find a fair price, VWAP is the obvious reference. It is not magic and it is not a guarantee — it is a well-watched level where orders tend to cluster.

ℹ️
Fair value is a description, not a forecast VWAP tells you where the average trade happened. It does not tell you where price will go next. Treat it as context, never as a prediction.

Price above vs below VWAP

The simplest read of VWAP is directional bias. Price above the line suggests buyers are in control of the session and that dips toward VWAP may attract support. Price below the line suggests sellers are in control and that rallies into VWAP may meet supply.

🟢 Price > VWAP Buyers have paid above the day's average. Dips toward VWAP are often watched as possible support.
🔴 Price < VWAP Sellers control the session. Rallies into VWAP may face supply and be watched as resistance.
➡️ Price ≈ VWAP Price hugging the line suggests balance — often a day of chop and low conviction.
◆ Trend day ◆ Balance day ◆ Reversion to VWAP ◆ VWAP bands

Anchored VWAP

A normal VWAP resets at the start of each session, which is handy intraday but blunt over weeks. Anchored VWAP solves that: you choose the starting point — an earnings gap, a swing high or low, a breakout, a news event — and the line accumulates from there.

The idea is to measure the average price paid since something important happened. If price stays above an anchored VWAP launched from a major swing low, the average buyer since that low is in profit — usually a sign of strength. Anchor from a swing high and staying below it can suggest persistent supply.

VWAP for entries and targets

In trending sessions, the most common use of VWAP is as a pullback reference. If price is above VWAP and the bias is bullish, traders often wait for a dip back toward the line before joining the trend, rather than chasing the highs. In a downtrend below VWAP, the mirror image: a rally into VWAP is watched as a possible place to sell.

  • Entry on the retest — price returns to VWAP and holds, offering a defined spot to act with a nearby invalidation point.
  • Target using VWAP — when in a counter-trend trade, VWAP can be a logical take-profit level, since it is where price often stalls.
  • VWAP bands — standard deviations around VWAP behave like envelopes, showing statistically extended moves and potential reversion zones.

None of these is guaranteed. VWAP is a reference that many participants share, which is exactly why it can work — and exactly why it can also be sliced through in a strong trend.

💡
Trade with the bias, not against the line Buying dips to VWAP in an up-trending session is a very different proposition from buying dips to VWAP in a downtrend. Use VWAP together with structure — structure first, VWAP as the fine-tuning level.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a VWAP touch as an automatic buy. Price crosses VWAP dozens of times in choppy sessions. Location and trend matter.
  • Ignoring the timeframe. A session VWAP on a 1-minute chart is noisy; anchored VWAP on a 4-hour chart is a structural reference. They are not interchangeable.
  • Using it on illiquid markets. With thin volume, small trades distort the line. VWAP works best where volume is deep and representative.
  • Forgetting the reset. A session VWAP means nothing tomorrow. If you want a multi-day reference, you need an anchored one.
  • Stacking five VWAP lines. Three anchors is already a lot of clutter. Pick the one event that matters.
⚠️
VWAP is a tool, not a system On its own, "above VWAP" is a weak filter. It becomes useful when it agrees with structure, momentum and a sensible risk plan — never when it is used alone.

Footprint Charts Explained: Reading Buying and Selling Pressure

A footprint chart cracks a candle open and shows the volume traded at every price inside it. Here is how to read the bid, the ask, delta and absorption.

10 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Most charts tell you what price did. A footprint chart tells you how it got there — how much volume traded at each individual price level inside a candle, and whether the aggression came from buyers or sellers. It is one of the deeper tools in the professional order-flow toolkit, and it rewards patience.

This guide is an honest introduction. We will cover what a footprint chart is, the two columns of bid and ask volume, delta, imbalance, absorption, how to read one step by step, and where — realistically — it fits alongside CPE Trade Pro's volume and order-block analysis. Educational content only; nothing here is financial advice.

What a footprint chart is

A standard candle compresses a whole period into four numbers: open, high, low and close. A footprint chart refuses to compress. For every candle, it lays out a small price ladder and shows how much volume traded at each price rung — and, crucially, it splits that volume into the two sides of the order book.

The result is a candle with its engine exposed. Instead of guessing whether a long wick was buyers rejecting a low or sellers being absorbed, you can see the numbers. Footprint charts are most common in futures and, increasingly, crypto, where trades are printed to a usable tick size.

ℹ️
Bid and ask are aggression, not identity "Ask volume" means trades that hit the offer — aggressive buying. "Bid volume" means trades that hit the bid — aggressive selling. Neither tells you who was on the other side.

The two columns: bid and ask

Every footprint row reads bid × ask. Bid volume is the market sell orders that traded into resting buy limit orders; ask volume is the market buy orders that lifted resting sell limits.

ColumnWhat it isYou read it as
BidVolume traded at the bidAggressive selling — sellers hitting resting buy orders
AskVolume traded at the askAggressive buying — buyers lifting resting sell orders
DeltaAsk minus bidNet aggressive pressure for that row or bar

Reading the columns together is where the skill lies. A row with 30 on the ask and 5 on the bid tells a different story from a row with 18 on each — the first is one-sided aggression, the second is a two-way battle.

Delta: the balance of pressure

Delta is simply ask volume minus bid volume. A positive delta means buyers were more aggressive; a negative delta means sellers were. Add it up across a whole session and you get cumulative delta — a running score of who has been winning the aggression battle over time.

Imbalance and stacked imbalances

When one side of the book dramatically outweighs the other at a price, traders call it an imbalance. A common rule of thumb is a 3:1 ratio — a row with 30 on the ask against 10 on the bid is a buy imbalance; flip it for a sell imbalance.

A single imbalanced row is interesting. Several imbalanced rows in a row — at a price the market is leaving behind quickly — is a stacked imbalance, and it usually marks a place where buyers or sellers were genuinely urgent. Stacked imbalances often line up with the fair value gaps and order blocks that structure-based traders already watch.

🔻 Bid imbalance Bid volume dwarfs ask volume — aggressive selling dominating that price.
🔺 Ask imbalance Ask volume dwarfs bid volume — aggressive buying dominating that price.
🧱 Stacked imbalance Several imbalanced rows in a row — urgent, one-sided participation.
◆ Ask imbalance ◆ Bid imbalance ◆ Stacked ◆ Absorption ◆ Exhaustion

Absorption explained

Absorption is the subtle one, and the most valuable. It happens when a huge amount of volume trades at a price — typically on one side — yet price barely moves. Someone is absorbing all that aggression with passive limit orders.

Picture a bar with a strongly positive delta (lots of aggressive buying) that fails to push higher. Buyers threw everything at it, and the sellers absorbed it. That is often a warning that the buying is exhausted and the move may reverse. The mirror image at a low — massive selling with no downward progress — suggests sellers are being absorbed.

💡
Absorption beats raw delta Big delta with little price movement is more informative than big delta with a big move. The first says "someone is stopping this"; the second just says "this is working".

How to read one step by step

  1. Start with the bar's overall shape. Where did it open and close, and where are the extremes?
  2. Find the busiest prices. High total volume marks where the market cared — often support or resistance.
  3. Check the bid/ask split. Is one side clearly dominant at those extremes, or is it balanced?
  4. Look for imbalances and stacks. A 3:1 row is a clue; a stack of them is a statement.
  5. Compute the delta. Net positive means buying led; net negative means selling led.
  6. Watch for absorption. Large volume that fails to move price is a warning flag.
  7. Tie it back to structure. Does the activity sit at an order block, a fair value gap or a key level? Alone, it is noise; in context, it is information.

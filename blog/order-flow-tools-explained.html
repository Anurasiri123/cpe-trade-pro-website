Order Flow Tools Explained: Reading the Real Market

Price tells you what happened. Order flow tries to tell you how and why. Here is the toolkit — footprint, delta, cumulative delta, volume profile and absorption.

11 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Every candle is a summary. It hides the argument that produced it — the buyers pushing, the sellers pushing back, and the volume that changed hands at each price along the way. Order flow is the discipline of reading that argument directly instead of inferring it from the shape of a candle.

This guide walks through the main order-flow tools, what each one actually shows, how they fit together, and where they sit honestly alongside CPE Trade Pro's volume and order-block analysis. Educational content only — nothing here is financial advice.

What order flow is

Order flow is the study of transactions: who was aggressive, at what price, and with how much size. Rather than asking "what did price do?", it asks "what were participants doing to make price do that?" A market order to buy that lifts the offer is aggressive buying; a market order to sell that hits the bid is aggressive selling. Order flow tools tally that aggression and show where it happened.

It is a deeper, more demanding layer of analysis than a moving average. It needs clean, granular data, and it rewards traders who are willing to slow down and read the tape of the market rather than react to a plotted line.

ℹ️
Depth, not certainty Order flow gives you more detail about the present. It does not make the future any more certain. Treat every tool here as a sharper lens, not a crystal ball.

Footprint, delta and cumulative delta

The foundation of order flow is the footprint — a candle that shows volume at each price, split into bid and ask. From there, two derived measures do most of the work:

  • Delta — ask volume minus bid volume for a bar or price row. Positive delta means aggressive buying led; negative means aggressive selling led.
  • Cumulative delta — the running total of delta through a session. It is a scoreboard of who has been winning the aggression battle over time.

The most useful pattern is a divergence. If price makes a new high but cumulative delta does not, buyers may be running out of steam even as price still climbs — the effort no longer matches the result.

Volume profile: POC, value area, HVN and LVN

A footprint looks at volume inside one bar. A volume profile looks at volume by price across a whole session or range, drawn as horizontal bars against the price axis. It answers a different question: at which prices did the market do most of its business?

  • POC (point of control) — the single price with the highest traded volume. It is where the market spent the most time, and often acts like a magnet.
  • Value area — the range that contains roughly 70% of the volume, with a value area high and value area low. Price inside the value area is "accepted"; outside it is exploring.
  • HVN (high volume node) — a bulky price level where trade was heavy. Price tends to slow and chop at high volume nodes.
  • LVN (low volume node) — a thin, fast level where little traded. Price tends to slice through low volume nodes quickly.

Absorption and exhaustion

These two words describe the ends of a move, and they are where order flow earns its keep.

Absorption is heavy volume with little price progress. Buyers (or sellers) throw size at a level, and passive limit orders soak it all up without the price moving. That is usually a sign that a large participant is defending a price — and a warning to anyone expecting the move to continue through it. Exhaustion is the flip side: the push keeps going but the delta behind it fades, so each new high (or low) is made on less aggression than the last.

💡
Where volume profile and order blocks meet A POC or HVN often sits right on top of an order block, and an LVN often sits inside a fair value gap. Different tools, same map — which is why they combine well.

Order flow vs indicators

Indicators are derived from price and volume after the fact — an RSI is a formula run over closes. Order flow looks at the raw transactions underneath. Neither replaces the other, and pretending one is "the answer" is how traders get into trouble.

The pragmatic approach is layered: use structure and indicators to know where you care, then use order flow to judge how price is behaving at that exact place.

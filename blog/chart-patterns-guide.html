Chart patterns: the shape of every reversal and continuation

Price does not move in straight lines. It leaves shapes behind — double tops, triangles, wedges, flags and more. Each shape is a story about who is in control. Learn to read the shape, and you read the market's next likely move.

12 min read Updated 2026 Category Technical analysis

A chart pattern is simply a recognisable shape that price draws as it swings up and down. Similar shapes appear across crypto, forex, stocks and metals because markets reflect changing supply, demand and participation. Patterns do not predict the future — they offer one possible interpretation of price structure. Always confirm with context and a candle close before making a decision.

1. Reversal patterns

Reversal patterns appear after a clear trend and suggest the move may be running out of steam. They are not automatic flip signals — wait for the break of the pattern's "neckline" or confirmation candle.

Double Top (M)

Two highs at nearly the same price, with a dip between them — shaped like the letter M. Buyers tried twice to push through one level and failed both times. The line across the middle dip is the neckline; a close below it is the trigger.

  • Bias: SELL
  • Entry: on a close below the neckline
  • Target idea: roughly the pattern's height, projected down from the neckline
  • Risk: the second top is often a FOMO spike — wait for the close, not the wick

Double Bottom (W)

The mirror image: two lows at nearly the same price, shaped like a W. Sellers failed twice to break one floor. A close above the middle peak (neckline) is the trigger.

  • Bias: BUY
  • Entry: on a close above the neckline
  • Target idea: pattern height projected up from the neckline

Head & Shoulders

Three peaks: a left shoulder, a higher middle "head", and a right shoulder roughly level with the left. The line connecting the two troughs is the neckline. This is the classic distribution pattern — buyers make one last higher high, then fail to repeat it.

  • Bias: SELL
  • Entry: close below the neckline
  • Target idea: head height projected below the neckline
  • Confluence: RSI often shows bearish divergence at the head

Inverse Head & Shoulders

The upside-down version at a bottom. A left shoulder low, a deeper head low, and a right shoulder low. A close above the neckline confirms accumulation.

  • Bias: BUY
  • Confluence: a Morning Star in the right-shoulder zone adds confidence
💡
Neckline rule Every double top/bottom and head-and-shoulders trade waits for a close beyond the neckline. A wick through the line is not a break — it is a trap.

Rounding Top & Bottom

A smooth, gradual curve instead of a sharp V. A rounding bottom (saucer) means selling fades slowly and buyers gradually take over. A rounding top (dome) means buying fades before price rolls over. These are slow — the message is that the losing side ran out of energy, not that one spike reversed everything.

  • Bias: rounding bottom = BUY; rounding top = SELL
  • Style: patience over many swings; take partials early on a rounding top

Triple Top & Triple Bottom

Three highs (or three lows) at nearly the same price. Three rejections at one wall means stubborn supply (or demand). Stronger than a double because the level has been tested more times. The neckline break is still the trigger.

2. Continuation patterns

Continuation patterns appear in the middle of a trend and suggest the trend is resting before resuming. They are not reversals — the small pullback is noise, not a change of heart.

Bull Flag & Bear Flag

A sharp move (the pole), then a small channel that drifts slightly against the trend (the flag), then a breakout in the original direction. The flag is a pause, not a reversal.

  • Bull flag: sharp rise → small downward channel → break up
  • Bear flag: sharp drop → small upward channel → break down
  • Target idea: the pole length, projected from the flag breakout
  • Mistake: buying the flag bounce before the breakout — you may be buying the worst price

Pennant

Like a flag but the pause is a tiny, tight triangle instead of a channel. Same idea: a sharp pole, a very short rest, then continuation. Measure the pole for a target idea. Never guess direction inside the tiny coil — wait for the break.

Cup & Handle

A rounded "U" (the cup) followed by a small, shallow pullback (the handle). It is a continuation pattern after a rest. The handle should be shallow and short — a deep handle means weak hands and a lower-quality setup. Enter on the handle breakout with a candle close.

Trend Channel

Price bounces between two roughly parallel lines. In an uptrend, buy near the lower channel line with a bullish candle; sell near the upper line with a bearish candle. In a downtrend, do the opposite. The middle of the channel is no-man's-land.

3. Compression patterns

These patterns are all about the market coiling. Price squeezes into a smaller and smaller range, and the eventual breakout is usually fast. The direction inside the pattern is unknown — trade the break, not the guess.

Triangle

Lower highs and higher lows converge into a point. This is the same lesson as a Bollinger Band squeeze: volatility is contracting and the calm will end. Do not predict direction inside the triangle — trade the close that breaks out of it.

Ascending Triangle

A flat ceiling (resistance) plus a rising floor (support). Buyers keep stepping up into a fixed resistance. Pressure builds against one price, and an upside breakout is common. Enter on a close above the ceiling.

Descending Triangle

A flat floor (support) plus a falling ceiling (resistance). Sellers keep pushing into a fixed support. A downside breakout is common. Enter on a close below the floor.

Rising Wedge & Falling Wedge

Both rails slope in the same direction but converge. The rising wedge looks bullish (price still rising) but the narrowing range means buyers are tiring — it often breaks down. The falling wedge looks bearish but often breaks up. A Hammer in the falling wedge tail is a classic confirmation.

  • Rising wedge: SELL bias — do not chase the last push up
  • Falling wedge: BUY bias — wait for the break + close

Rectangle (Range)

Price bounces between a flat ceiling and a flat floor — a sideways box. Fade the edges inside the range, and trade the break outside it. Most Bollinger Band squeezes sit inside a rectangle.

⚠️
Inside the pattern, direction is a guess Triangles, wedges and rectangles compress price. The breakout direction is unknown until a candle closes outside. Trading inside the coil is gambling; trading the breakout is strategy.

4. Advanced & rare patterns

Broadening Formation

Higher highs and lower lows — the range fans out instead of pinching. This is a chaotic, emotional market where stops get hit both ways. Smaller size or stand aside; it often ends in a violent one-way move.

Diamond

Wide in the middle, narrow at both ends — like a triangle that morphs then un-morphs. A classic topping or bottoming formation. Rare and slow, so most bars inside feel like noise. Be patient; the breakout direction decides.

Island Reversal

Price gaps away, trades in a lonely cluster, then gaps back — leaving an "island" of trapped traders. The gaps are the signal, not the island candles. More common in stocks and forex than crypto.

Harmonic Patterns

A measured zig-zag (X-A-B-C-D) where each leg has a Fibonacci ratio — Gartley, Bat, Crab and similar styles. The D point is the entry zone, defined by ratios, not feelings. This is Fibonacci taken one level deeper — master plain Fibonacci first.

5. How to use patterns without getting trapped

  • Start with structure. Is the market trending or ranging? Patterns mean different things in each. CPE Trade Pro's SMC overlay labels BOS and CHoCH so you see context first.
  • Wait for the close. A wick through a neckline or triangle rail is not a break. A candle close is.
  • Confirm with a candle. A pattern breakout plus a strong engulfing or marubozu candle is far more reliable than the break alone.
  • Size from the stop. The pattern height tells you where to place the stop. A bigger pattern means a bigger stop — shrink your size accordingly.
  • Every pattern can fail. Patterns describe a leaning, not a promise. Always have an invalidation point.

Chart drawing tools explained: trend line, ray, extended line, horizontal, zone and R:R

Drawing tools are not decoration. Each one answers a specific question — where is the trend, where did price react, what do I risk and what do I aim for. Here is what every tool in the CPE Trade Pro chart is actually for.

11 min read Updated 2026 Category Tools

There is a version of charting where the screen fills up with lines until it looks like a weather map. That is not analysis; it is clutter. The tools below are useful because each one draws a single, checkable fact — "price keeps turning here", "the trend has held since this point" — that you can act on or reject.

The golden rule is restraint: a clean chart with two or three meaningful marks beats a busy one with twenty. Before you draw anything, ask what question the line is answering.

Why draw at all

Drawing forces you to be specific. "It looks bullish" becomes "price is holding above this trend line and the last swing high". A specific claim can be tested; a feeling cannot. The best drawings are also the ones you can explain to someone else in one sentence.

ℹ️
Draw with a purpose If you cannot say what a line is for, delete it. Lines you do not remember drawing are lines you will not respect when price reaches them.

Trend line

A trend line is a straight line joining two or more swing points, drawn between those points only. An uptrend line is drawn under rising lows; a downtrend line over falling highs. It works because it shows where buyers (or sellers) have repeatedly stepped in.

Three rules keep a trend line honest: it needs at least two touches to exist, a third touch to become meaningful, and it should be drawn against the wicks or the bodies — pick one and stay consistent. A line forced through a single point is just a guess with a straightedge.

Ray vs extended line

These two look like a trend line but behave differently, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion:

  • Ray — starts at one point and continues indefinitely in one direction. Use it when you want a level that begins at a specific moment (a breakout point, a pivot) and matters into the future only.
  • Extended line — continues indefinitely in both directions. Use it when you believe a level is real on both sides of your anchor point — for example a channel you expect price to respect forwards and backwards.

Horizontal line for support and resistance

A horizontal line is the simplest and often the most useful mark on a chart. Unlike a trend line it does not slope, so it marks a single price level where the market has repeatedly turned — a support below price or a resistance above it.

Two habits make horizontals better. First, mark zones rather than razor-thin lines: support and resistance are ranges, not exact pennies, and a thin line invites false precision. Second, keep only the levels price is likely to care about now — the nearest support under you and the nearest resistance over you. A chart with twenty horizontals means none of them stand out.

Zones: supply, demand and order blocks

A zone is a rectangle marking a price area where something important happened — a strong rejection (supply), a strong launch (demand), or an order block, the last opposing candle before a big move. Zones are the practical version of support and resistance because they have width and they show intent.

The risk-reward (R:R) tool

The risk-reward tool is the one drawing that pays for itself. You mark an entry, a stop and a target, and the tool shows the two distances as a ratio. If you risk 100 to make 300, that is a 3:1 — usually written 3R.

The point is not to demand a huge ratio. It is to make the trade's shape visible before you take it. A setup with a wide stop and a near target is a bad trade no matter how handsome the signal looks; the R:R box shows that in one glance, which is exactly when you still have time to walk away.

💡
Draw the stop first Always place the stop level before the target. It is easier to accept a ratio once you have decided what you are unwilling to lose, and it stops you from talking yourself into a bad shape.

A clean chart in practice

Here is a workflow that keeps a chart readable:

  1. Mark the nearest support and resistance as zones, not lines.
  2. Draw one trend line on the timeframe you are actually trading.
  3. Add a ray only if a single anchor point matters to you.
  4. Box the zone you would trade from — demand, supply or an order block.
  5. Run the R:R tool on the plan to see if the shape is worth taking.
  6. Delete everything else. If a mark is older than your reason for watching, it is noise.

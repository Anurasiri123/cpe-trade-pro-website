Fair value gaps (FVG) explained

A fair value gap is a small piece of your chart where price moved so fast that one side barely traded. Traders watch these gaps because price often comes back to them. Here is what they are, how to spot them, and how to use them without over-trading.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Technical analysis

If an order block shows you where large orders were filled, a fair value gap shows you where price skipped through a level too quickly. Both are Smart Money Concepts tools, and they are often used together. This guide focuses on the fair value gap: what it is, how to recognise it, when it tends to get filled, and how it differs from an order block.

💡
Short version A fair value gap is a three-candle pattern where the wicks of candle 1 and candle 3 do not overlap. The empty space between them is the gap — and price often revisits it later.

1. What is a fair value gap?

A fair value gap (FVG), also called an imbalance, is an area on the chart where buying and selling were not balanced. It forms during a fast move, when price travels through a range so quickly that it does not trade evenly on the way.

In practical terms, an FVG is the space left between the wicks of the candles that made the move. Because price moved so fast, that area is seen as “unfair” — one side of the market did not get a fair chance to trade. Many traders expect the market to return to that space later, filling in the imbalance before continuing.

2. The three-candle imbalance

The cleanest way to spot an FVG is with a three-candle pattern:

  1. Look at candle 1 and candle 3.
  2. Check whether their wicks overlap.
  3. If they do not overlap, the space between them is the fair value gap.

The middle candle (candle 2) is the one that did all the work — it moved so strongly that it left a gap between the candles on either side. A gap like this is only meaningful when the middle candle is part of a genuine displacement, not a random quiet candle.

3. Bullish vs bearish FVG

The pattern mirrors itself depending on direction:

  • Bullish FVG — the low of candle 3 sits completely above the high of candle 1. The gap sits below current price and is usually treated as a demand area.
  • Bearish FVG — the high of candle 3 sits completely below the low of candle 1. The gap sits above current price and is usually treated as a supply area.
FeatureBullish FVGBearish FVG
Which wicks do not overlapLow of candle 3 above high of candle 1High of candle 3 below low of candle 1
Direction of the moveStrong upStrong down
Where the gap sitsBelow priceAbove price
Typical roleDemand / supportSupply / resistance
Common trade ideaBuy on the pullback into the gapSell on the bounce into the gap

4. Why a gap forms

Gaps form because of speed. When a lot of buying (or selling) hits the market at once, price jumps through levels instead of trading through them candle by candle. The result is a stretch on the chart where very few trades took place.

That is why displacement matters. A slow, grinding move tends to leave balanced price action with no gaps. A sharp, urgent move leaves gaps behind. Traders read those gaps as a sign that institutional-size orders were active — the same reason order blocks form.

⚠️
Size matters A tiny gap on a noisy chart is not useful. Mark the gaps that are clearly tied to a strong move, and skip the ones that look like normal market noise.

5. When price fills a gap — and when it does not

Not every gap gets filled, and no gap is guaranteed. A few general tendencies are worth knowing:

  • Trending markets may leave gaps unfilled. If demand is strong, price can keep running and never return to the gap.
  • Pullbacks often reach the nearest gap first. The closest unfilled gap is the most likely area to be tested.
  • Partial fills are common. Price often enters the gap and reverses before fully filling it — traders sometimes call this a “50% fill”.
  • Gaps lose value once filled. After price trades through the whole gap, it is no longer an imbalance.

Because there is no guarantee of a fill, an FVG should be treated as a location of interest, not a promise. You still need a reason to act — a reaction candle, a structure shift, or confluence with another level.

6. FVG vs order block

Fair value gaps and order blocks are closely related — both come from displacement — but they are not the same thing:

AspectOrder block (OB)Fair value gap (FVG)
What it isThe last opposite candle before the moveThe empty space between candle wicks
How it is foundStepping back from the displacementOverlap check across three candles
What it representsWhere orders were likely filledWhere price moved too fast to trade evenly
Zone shapeA candle body or full-candle boxThe gap between wicks
Typical useEntry zone on a retestEntry area or target for a retrace

In practice, the strongest setups are where an order block and an FVG sit at the same price. That overlap gives you two independent reasons to expect a reaction in one zone.

7. Combining FVG with structure

An FVG on its own is just a gap. It becomes a trade idea when it lines up with structure:

  • Direction first. Decide the trend with swings, BOS and CHoCH on a higher timeframe.
  • Then location. Look for an FVG that sits in the direction of that trend.
  • Then timing. Wait for price to reach the gap and show a reaction before acting.

A bullish FVG below price in an uptrend is a pullback location. The same gap in a strong downtrend is far less interesting. Structure gives the gap meaning.

9. A worked example (described, not priced)

Picture a chart where price has been drifting sideways. Suddenly a wide green candle appears: it opens near its low and closes near its high. The candle before it is a small red candle, and the candle after it is also green.

Now line up the three candles. The low of the third candle sits above the high of the first candle, so the wicks do not overlap — a bullish fair value gap has formed. The gap is the empty slice between those two wicks.

Price continues higher for a while, then pulls back. As it drifts down, it reaches the top of the gap and slows. This is the moment the gap becomes a decision point: either buyers step in and price bounces, or price pushes deeper and fills more of the gap. Either way, the trader who marked the gap in advance has a location to watch — instead of reacting to every candle on the screen. That is the whole point of the concept.

10. Common mistakes

  • Marking every gap. Noisy markets create tiny gaps constantly. Only the ones tied to real displacement matter.
  • Expecting every gap to fill. Strong trends often leave gaps behind. A gap is a possibility, not a promise.
  • Ignoring direction. A gap against the higher-timeframe trend is a low-quality idea.
  • Entering without confirmation. A gap is a location. Wait for a reaction before you act.
  • Forgetting the stop. If price closes through the gap, the idea is wrong — plan the invalidation before you enter.

11. Summary

A fair value gap is a simple idea: a fast move leaves an empty space between candles, and price often returns to that space later. Learn to spot the three-candle pattern, mark only the gaps that come from real displacement, and combine them with structure, order blocks and strict risk control. Treated as a location to watch rather than a guarantee, the FVG becomes a genuinely useful tool.

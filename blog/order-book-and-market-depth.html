Order Book, DOM and Market Depth Explained

Price does not move on its own — it moves because orders meet. Here is what the order book, the DOM ladder, market depth, liquidity walls and heatmaps actually show, and where each one ends.

11 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

A candlestick shows you the aftermath of a trade. It tells you what price did over a period, but not the queue of orders that made it do so. The order book is that queue: the resting buy and sell orders waiting to be matched, laid out price by price. Reading it is one of the closest things to watching the negotiation itself.

This guide walks through the order book, the depth-of-market ladder, market depth and liquidity walls, heatmaps, and the important distinction between legitimate iceberg orders and illegal spoofing. It also covers, honestly, what CPE Trade Pro's Order Book and Walls view does and does not do. Educational content only — nothing here is financial advice.

What the order book is

An order book is two lists. The bids are limit orders to buy, each at a specific price; the asks (or offers) are limit orders to sell. The highest bid and the lowest ask sit closest to the middle. The difference between them is the spread.

The spread matters more than beginners expect. On a liquid pair at a major exchange it is often a single tick, so the cost of crossing it is tiny. In a thin market it can widen to several ticks, and every trade starts slightly behind. A narrow spread and thick size at the top of the book are the first honest signs of real liquidity — a theme that runs through our separate guide to liquidity in trading.

Crucially, an order book is a snapshot of intent, not commitment. Nothing stops a resting order being cancelled. That single fact is why every tool below needs confirming with actual trades.

The DOM ladder (depth of market)

Depth of market — the DOM ladder — takes the order book and turns it vertical: price down the middle, resting size beside it. The top rows show the asks, the bottom rows show the bids, and the middle row is the spread. Traders watch the ladder to see how much size is queued at each price and how quickly that queue appears and disappears.

The ladder is a measuring device, not a prophecy. A large resting bid looks supportive until it is pulled; the only thing that truly confirms a level is a trade printing there, or the conspicuous absence of one. Read the ladder as live measurement of intent, never as a promise.

Market depth and liquidity walls

Market depth is the total resting size at, and away from, the best price — a measure of how much the market can absorb before price has to move. When a single price holds a disproportionate amount of that size, it forms a wall. A genuine wall can slow price because trades must eat through it to pass; a decorative one means nothing.

Depth is best read as a shape, not a number. A book that stays thick across several prices on both sides can take size without moving; a book that thins out sharply a few ticks away will slip fast on any real order. Watch how the shape changes as price approaches a level, not just the size sitting there now.

⚠️
A wall is not a promise Resting size can vanish the instant before price arrives — especially on venues where cancelling is cheap. Never treat a wall as guaranteed support or resistance. Wait for price and trade flow to confirm it before you act.

Heatmaps

A heatmap plots resting liquidity across both price and time. Each column is a moment, each row is a price, and the shade of each cell shows how much size was queued there. Bright bands that persist across many columns are levels the market keeps defending; bright cells that flash and fade are usually fleeting orders.

Heatmaps are visually powerful and slightly dangerous. They only show resting orders, so they flatter limit orders and hide the aggressive taker flow that actually moves price. Use them to locate where interest sits, then confirm with the traded volume — the approach covered in our order flow tools guide and footprint charts guide.

Iceberg orders and spoofing

Two order-book behaviours are worth naming because they explain a lot of confusing depth. An iceberg order shows only a small tip of its true size; as the visible slice fills, another appears, so the level keeps refilling. It is a legitimate way for a large participant to hide their footprint and avoid spooking the market.

Spoofing is different, and it is not legitimate. It means placing large orders you never intend to fill, purely to create a false impression of supply or demand, then cancelling them once others react. Spoofing is illegal in regulated markets and, crucially, unreliable to trade against — because the thing you are reading is a lie by design.

  • Iceberg: real size, partially hidden; the level genuinely holds and refills as it trades.
  • Spoof: fake size, all visible, then pulled; it never fills and rarely stays put.
  • The tell: watch what happens when price reaches the level. Real size trades or refills; fake size disappears.

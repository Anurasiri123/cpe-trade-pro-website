Fundamental Analysis for Traders: Central Banks, NFP and CPI

Charts show what price did. Fundamentals help explain why. Here is a plain-English tour of rates, central banks, data releases and intermarket moves — and how sentiment folds into a quality score.

11 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Most of the material on this site is about reading a chart. But charts do not move on their own — behind every candle there is a reason: a rate decision, an inflation number, a shift in sentiment about a currency or a coin. Fundamental analysis is the study of those reasons. It will not time your entries, but it gives context that makes technical setups easier to trust or to doubt.

This guide is a plain-English tour of what matters. We cover rates and central banks, the data releases that move markets fastest, intermarket relationships, crypto-specific fundamentals and how a sentiment read can sit alongside technical signals. Educational content only, nothing here is financial advice.

What fundamental analysis is

Fundamental analysis asks what a market should be worth based on the economic forces around it. In Forex that means comparing two economies — their interest rates, inflation, growth and employment — to judge which currency is likely to strengthen. In crypto it means looking at supply schedules, adoption, regulation and network activity rather than a single company's earnings.

For an active trader, fundamentals are a bias tool, not a timing tool. They tell you which side of the market the odds currently favour, and they warn you when a scheduled event could tear through your technical setup. The chart then does the job of timing the entry. Using the two together is far stronger than leaning on either alone.

💡
Check the calendar before you trade A clean technical setup entered minutes before a major data release is a gamble, not a plan. Know what is due and when — then decide whether to be in the market for it.

Interest rates and central banks

Interest rates are the single biggest driver in FX, and often in crypto too. When a central bank raises rates, holding that currency becomes more attractive, which tends to strengthen it; when it cuts, the opposite. Traders watch not just the decision but the tone around it — whether the bank sounds hawkish (leaning to higher rates) or dovish (leaning to cuts).

The four banks that matter most are the Federal Reserve (US dollar), the European Central Bank (euro), the Bank of England (pound) and the Bank of Japan (yen). Each meets on a published schedule and publishes a statement, and sometimes a press conference. The words can move price more than the number, because markets trade expectations: a "hold" seen as dovish can weaken a currency even when the rate did not change.

Monetary policy is the tool kit behind those decisions — moving the policy rate, and in some eras buying bonds (quantitative easing) or shrinking a balance sheet. You do not need to forecast policy to trade. You need to know which way the wind is blowing and to expect volatility when the wind might change.

Key data releases

NFP, the US non-farm payrolls report, lands on the first Friday of most months and shows how many jobs the economy added. CPI, the consumer price index, measures inflation and now carries extra weight because it feeds directly into rate expectations. GDP shows headline growth, and FOMC meetings and their minutes reveal the Fed's thinking.

What matters for a trader is the gap between the forecast and the actual number, and the direction of surprise, not the raw figure. A CPI print hotter than expected tends to push rate-cut hopes back, which supports the currency; a weak payrolls number can do the reverse. Because these releases often hit within seconds, spreads widen and price can spike both ways before settling — another reason to have a plan before, not during.

Intermarket analysis

Markets do not move in isolation. Intermarket analysis watches the relationships between them. The US Dollar Index (DXY) is the easiest gauge of broad dollar strength and often moves inversely to gold and major commodity currencies. Bonds and their yields reflect what the market expects from rates; rising yields tend to support a currency. Gold is watched both as a hedge and as a real-rates barometer.

You do not need to trade all of these. The practical use is confirmation. If you are bullish on the dollar for technical reasons and DXY is also pressing higher while gold softens, the fundamental and technical pictures agree — a stronger read. When they conflict, that is a signal to size down or stand aside rather than to force the trade.

Crypto fundamentals

Crypto has its own fundamental drivers. The most famous is the halving — a scheduled event that cuts the reward for mining new Bitcoin roughly every four years, slowing the growth of supply. ETFs and other regulated products can change who is able to buy, shifting demand. Below the surface, on-chain data covers activity like active addresses, exchange flows and the behaviour of large holders.

Sentiment has its own quick thermometer in the Crypto Fear & Greed Index, which bundles volatility, momentum, social buzz and other inputs into a single number from extreme fear to extreme greed. It is a useful mood gauge, not a forecast: extremes can mark turning points, but they can also persist far longer than anyone expects. Treat it as one clue among many, and be careful never to let a sentiment reading replace your own risk management.

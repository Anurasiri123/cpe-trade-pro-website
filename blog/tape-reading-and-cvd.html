Tape Reading and CVD Explained: Following the Real Buyers and Sellers

The order book shows orders waiting. The tape shows orders that actually happened. Here is how to read time & sales, aggressor flow, delta and cumulative volume delta.

11 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Every trade that prints is a small confession. Someone chose to act now, at that price, with that size. The tape — time and sales — is the unedited record of those confessions, a second-by-second stream of price, size and the side that was in a hurry. Reading it separates "who is waiting" from "who is trading".

This guide covers what the tape is, how to read aggressor flow, delta and cumulative volume delta (CVD), the tell-tale patterns of absorption and divergence, and how to combine the tape with footprint charts and VWAP. Educational content only — nothing here is financial advice.

What the tape is (time & sales)

The tape is a scrolling list of every executed trade: the time, the price and the size. On most platforms each print is also tagged by which side was the aggressor — a trade that hits the bid is a market sell, a trade that lifts the ask is a market buy. That tag is the whole point: it tells you who crossed the spread to get the trade done.

Beginners watch price; the tape rewards watching size. A stream of small prints means little. A burst of large prints at one price, all on the same side, is someone with real conviction acting right now. The tape is noisy by nature, so you read it in clusters, not one print at a time.

Reading aggressor flow

Aggressor flow is the balance of that tagging over a short window. If most prints are lifting the offer, buyers are in a hurry and price usually drifts up; if most are hitting the bid, sellers are in control. The useful skill is not counting ticks but spotting where the size concentrates — at which price a wall of green or red prints keeps appearing.

Context changes everything, though. Heavy buying into a level that refuses to break is not strength; it may be sellers quietly absorbing every buy. That is why aggressor flow is read alongside the price response, never on its own.

💡
Watch the effort, then the result Compare how much aggression appears with how far price actually moves. Big effort that produces little movement is the most informative thing the tape can show you — and it points straight to absorption.

Delta and cumulative volume delta (CVD)

Delta turns the tape into a single number: aggressive buy volume minus aggressive sell volume, for a bar or a price row. Positive delta means buyers led; negative means sellers led. Cumulative volume delta (CVD) simply adds that up trade by trade through the session, producing a running scoreboard of which side has been the more aggressive.

CVD is powerful because it can be compared with price. When price and CVD move together, aggression and result agree. When they part ways, you are looking at a divergence — the first warning that the current move is losing its engine.

Absorption and divergence

Absorption is the tape's quietest message. It happens when aggressive orders pour in but price barely moves, because a passive counterparty is soaking them up. Buyers lift the offer again and again, yet the price stalls — someone larger is selling into every print without budging the level.

Divergence is absorption seen over time. Price makes a new high, but CVD does not; or price makes a new low, but CVD holds firm. In both cases the effort no longer matches the result, and the trend is running on fumes rather than fuel. Note the word "warning" — divergence can resolve with a reversal or with a burst that repairs it.

📈 Agreement Price and CVD rise together. Aggression and result match — the move is healthy.
🧲 Absorption Big delta, flat price. A passive counterparty is eating the aggression.
↔️ Divergence Price and CVD make opposite extremes. Effort no longer matches result.

Combining tape with footprint and VWAP

No single tool is complete. The tape tells you the pace and side of executions; a footprint chart tells you at which prices they happened; and VWAP gives you the average price participants have paid, which is a natural line of reference for whether buyers or sellers are in profit on the day.

A practical stack: use VWAP to set the daily context, the footprint to see where volume and imbalance sit at each price, and the tape to judge who is currently winning that price. When all three agree — price above VWAP, ask-heavy footprint at an old level, and steady buying on the tape — you have a coherent story. When they disagree, that disagreement is the signal to slow down. For the wider toolkit, see our order flow tools guide.

Practise on live, quiet markets before you rely on any of this. Reading the tape is a skill, not a setting.

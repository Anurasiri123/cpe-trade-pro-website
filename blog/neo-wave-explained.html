Neo Wave Explained: The Next Step After Elliott Wave

Elliott Wave gave us a map but left a lot to the imagination. Neo Wave adds rules, time and structure. Here is what it is, how it differs, and where it is genuinely useful.

11 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

If you have read about Elliott Wave, you know the complaint: two analysts, one chart, two counts, and no clear way to say which is wrong. Neo Wave was created to fix exactly that. It keeps the core idea — markets move in repeating wave structures — but replaces Elliott's loose guidelines with hard, testable rules.

This guide is an honest introduction: what Neo Wave is, who built it, how its rules differ from Elliott's guidelines, the mono-wave and action/reaction vocabulary, why timing matters, and how complexity levels organise the whole thing. Educational content only — no method here predicts markets, and none of it is financial advice.

What Neo Wave is

Neo Wave was developed by Glenn Neely and laid out in his 1990 book Mastering Elliott Wave. Neely spent years trying to apply Elliott's framework systematically and kept running into the same wall: too many valid-looking counts. His response was to rebuild the method around logic rather than intuition.

The result is a stricter, more mechanical approach. Where classic Elliott tells you what a healthy impulse "usually" looks like, Neo Wave tells you what a structure must do and, just as importantly, what would prove it is not that structure. That shift from description to testable rule is the defining feature of Neo Wave — and the reason some traders find it more disciplined than the original.

Logic rules vs Elliott's guidelines

Elliott Wave has three "unbreakable" rules but treats almost everything else as a guideline: corrections usually retrace this much, wave three is typically longest, and so on. That flexibility is friendly to read and dangerous to trade, because almost any chart can be made to fit something.

Neo Wave turns as much of this as possible into logic rules — statements that can be checked against the chart and either hold or fail. Neely also introduces behaviour and time rules that Elliott never specified. The practical upshot: Neo Wave counts get invalidated more often, and that is the point. A method that tells you when you are wrong is worth more than one that always has an excuse.

More rules means fewer trades Neo Wave throws out many counts that Elliott would accept. That filters out weak setups — but it also means you will sit on your hands a lot more. Discipline is the trade-off.

Mono-waves, action and reaction

Neo Wave starts from the smallest useful unit: the mono-wave, a single uninterrupted directional push in price with no meaningful pull-back inside it. Every larger structure is assembled from these basic legs, which makes the whole method easier to reason about from the ground up.

Each mono-wave is labelled as either action or reaction. Action is movement in the direction of the larger trend; reaction is the counter-move against it. A simple impulse is then an alternating chain — action, reaction, action, reaction — and the balance between the two tells you something about the strength of the trend. Strong trends show long, energetic action legs and short, tired reactions.

➡️ Action A mono-wave moving with the larger trend. Energetic and long in a strong trend.
⬅️ Reaction The counter-trend pull-back between action legs. Short and shallow when the trend is healthy.
🧩 Structure Action and reaction legs stack into impulses, corrections and larger nested patterns.

Time vs price — the part Elliott ignores

Classic Elliott Wave is almost purely a study of price ratios. Neo Wave adds the fourth dimension that most wave theory skips: time. Two moves that travel the same number of points are not equal if one took an hour and the other took a week.

Neely argues that the manner of a move — how directly it travels, how long each leg lasts, whether the legs alternate in duration — is part of the pattern, not noise around it. So Neo Wave includes timing and behaviour rules that a valid structure should satisfy. This is harder to eyeball than a Fibonacci ratio, which is exactly why it is usually left to software and careful measurement rather than quick screen-gazing.

Wave complexity levels

Neo Wave also organises structures by complexity. Rather than memorising dozens of named patterns, you move up a ladder: the simplest single mono-wave, then basic action-and-reaction pairs, then the first full impulse and correction structures, then progressively more involved combinations as the scale grows. Each level builds on the one below.

Thinking in levels keeps you honest about scale. A structure that looks like a completed pattern on a 15-minute chart is usually a single piece of something much simpler — or much larger — on the daily. Neo Wave's rules apply at each level, so the same logic that validates a small correction also constrains the bigger pattern it sits inside.

How it improves on classic Elliott

It is worth being clear about what Neo Wave does and does not fix. It does not make wave analysis predictive or profitable on its own. What it does is reduce the wiggle room. By insisting on rules that can fail, it gives you a cleaner way to decide that a count is dead and it is time to stand aside.

  • Falsifiable counts. A rule break invalidates the structure instead of inviting a re-label, which cuts out a lot of self-deception.
  • Time included. Timing and behaviour rules capture information that pure price ratios throw away.
  • Structured from the ground up. Mono-waves and complexity levels make the method consistent from the smallest leg to the largest pattern.
  • Still a framework, not a forecast. Different valid structures can coexist until price resolves, and Neo Wave does not change that fundamental truth.
⚠️
Rigour is not certainty Neo Wave raises the bar for what counts as a valid structure, but it cannot tell you what price will do next. Treat every count as a hypothesis with an invalidation level, and size risk so that being wrong is ordinary, not catastrophic.

RSI Explained: Reading Momentum Without Guessing

The Relative Strength Index is on almost every chart and misread on almost every chart. Here is what it actually measures, why "overbought" is not a sell button, how divergence works, and where RSI sits inside the CPE Trade Pro momentum score.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Few indicators are quoted as confidently as RSI, and few are understood as loosely. Traders say "RSI is overbought, it has to come down" as though the indicator carried a rule. It does not. RSI is a momentum gauge — a speedometer, not a brake — and reading it well means understanding what it compares and what it cannot know.

What RSI measures

The Relative Strength Index, developed by J. Welles Wilder, compares the size of recent gains to recent losses and squeezes the result onto a 0 to 100 scale. In plain terms: how much of the recent move was up versus down. A reading high on the scale means up-moves have been bigger and more frequent; a reading low on the scale means the opposite.

The default period is 14 bars. That is not magic — it is a balance between reacting quickly and not jumping at every candle. The indicator is bounded: it can never go above 100 or below 0, and it tends to stay in a narrower band in practice.

Crucially, RSI is relative. It says nothing about the price level itself — only about the balance of recent strength. A coin at $10 and the same coin at $100 can both print RSI 55.

The 0-100 scale (figure)

Most platforms draw RSI in its own panel below price, with horizontal guides at 70 and 30 (sometimes 80 and 20). Price runs up, the RSI line follows the momentum up, and vice versa.

Overbought and oversold — the myth

Above 70 is labelled "overbought" and below 30 "oversold", and the labels trip people up. They sound like instructions. They are not. They mean recent gains (or losses) have been unusually large and one-sided — nothing about what happens next.

In a strong trend, RSI can stay above 70 for days. Selling simply because it crossed 70 is one of the most common ways to fight a market that is not done. The more useful reading is behaviour around the band: does RSI stall there and roll over, or keep pushing? A trend that keeps RSI pinned high is showing strength, not danger.

⚠️
"Overbought" is not "sell" The label describes what has already happened, not a forecast. Treat 70 and 30 as reference lines to interpret, never as buttons to press.

Divergence: the useful part

Divergence is where RSI earns its place on the chart. It happens when price and RSI disagree. In bearish divergence, price makes a higher high but RSI makes a lower high — the move is stretching while its momentum fades. In bullish divergence, price makes a lower low but RSI makes a higher low — the selling is running out of fuel.

Divergence is a warning to pay attention, not a reversal order. It frequently appears several times before anything happens, and in strong trends it can be ignored for a long while. Its best use is as a reason to tighten how you manage a position, not to jump the other way blind.

Settings that matter

Almost every RSI argument is really an argument about settings. The three that matter:

  • Length (14). Shorter = faster and noisier; longer = smoother and slower. 14 is the reference used by most traders, so it is the one to compare against.
  • Source. Almost always the close. Change it only if you have a specific reason.
  • Bands (70/30). Tighter bands trigger more often and mean less; wider bands trigger rarely. Stick with the defaults unless you are deliberately testing.

Common mistakes

  • Selling every 70. Overbought in a trend is strength, not a sell order.
  • Trading divergence blindly. Divergence can appear repeatedly before price reacts — wait for structure to agree.
  • Using RSI as a trend tool. RSI tracks momentum, not direction; it lags on trend changes.
  • Ignoring the timeframe. A 4-hour overbought reading outranks a 5-minute one; do not mix them.
  • Changing the period to fit. If 14 is not working, a different number usually just reshapes the same story.

SMA explained: the 50 simple moving average

The simple moving average is the plainest line on any chart — an honest average of the last n closes. The 50 period version is one of the most followed, and used well it is a simple trend filter rather than a signal.

9 min read Updated 2026 Category Indicators

If the exponential moving average is the fast, twitchy cousin, the simple moving average is the steady one. It gives every bar in its window equal weight, so it drifts calmly behind price instead of snapping at it. That calmness is exactly why the 50-period SMA has become a default trend line for so many traders.

This guide covers what the SMA is, how it is calculated, why the 50 stands out, the difference between price above and below the line, and how it feeds into CPE Trade Pro's analysis.

1. What an SMA is

A simple moving average takes the last n closing prices, adds them up, and divides by n. Every bar counts the same. As each new candle closes, the oldest one drops out of the window and the newest joins, so the average slides forward one step at a time — hence "moving".

Because it smooths price, the SMA filters out the jitter of individual candles and shows the direction of the recent average. It lags by design: a longer average is smoother but slower to turn, a shorter one is quicker but noisier. That single trade-off is the whole art of choosing a length.

2. How it is calculated

An SMA 50 at any bar is simply the average of the last 50 closes. Picture a 50-bar window sliding along the chart one candle at a time: each new close changes the total, and the average is rewritten.

3. SMA 50 as a trend filter

The most common way the SMA 50 is used is not as a signal but as a filter. Before taking any trade, many traders simply ask: is price above the 50 or below it? Above, they look for longs. Below, they look for shorts — or, on a long-only market, they stand aside.

This is a crude switch, and that is its value. It prevents the classic mistake of buying into a downtrend or shorting a rally, because it forces you to line your idea up with the average's direction first.

💡
A filter, not a trigger "Above the 50" does not mean buy. It means the trend permission is green — you still need a reason to enter, a level and a stop.

4. Price above vs below

The relationship between price and the SMA 50 is a quick read on the recent trend.

What you seePlain reading
Price above a rising SMA 50Recent prices stronger than the average and the average is climbing — a healthy uptrend
Price above a falling SMA 50A bounce inside a downtrend; the average has not turned yet
Price below a falling SMA 50Recent prices weaker than the average and the average is dropping — a downtrend
Price crossing the SMA 50 back and forthNo clear trend; the filter gives mixed readings

The slope matters as much as the side. Price above a rising average is a much stronger signal than price above a flat one.

↑price above, average rising — healthy uptrend ↗price above, average falling — a bounce ↓price below, average falling — downtrend ±price crossing back and forth — no clear trend

5. SMA vs EMA

The simple average weights every bar equally; the exponential average weights recent bars more. The practical difference is speed versus smoothness.

  • SMA is smoother — it ignores short bursts and turns later, which suits slow, structural trend reading.
  • EMA is faster — it turns sooner, which suits shorter timeframes and quicker decisions, at the cost of more false turns.

Neither is "correct". Many traders keep one of each: a slow SMA for the big-picture filter and a faster EMA for timing. If you want the detail, it is covered in EMA explained.

6. The 50 / 200 crossovers

Two longer averages are watched more than any others: the 50 and the 200. When the faster 50 crosses above the slower 200, it is nicknamed a golden cross; when it crosses below, a death cross. They are simply long-term summaries of trend change.

7. Common mistakes

  • Using the SMA as a trigger. "Above the 50" is permission, not an entry. You still need a setup and a stop.
  • Ignoring the slope. A flat average gives mixed signals; the direction of the line is half the story.
  • Expecting the average to hold as support. Price can pass through any moving average.
  • Reacting to a golden or death cross too late. Long averages turn slowly; by the time they cross, much of the move may be done.
  • Stacking too many averages. More lines do not mean more clarity — often just more clutter.

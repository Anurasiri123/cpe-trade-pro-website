Elliott Wave Explained: Impulse, Correction and Counts

Markets do not move in straight lines — they move in waves. Here is the 5-3 pattern, the three rules that must hold, and how CPE Trade Pro shows a light wave count.

10 min read Updated 2026 Category Learn

Elliott Wave is one of the oldest ways of looking at market structure. Instead of treating price as random noise, it argues that crowd behaviour repeats in recognisable patterns — a push in the trend, then a pull-back, again and again, at every scale. Learn the shape and you gain a language for describing where price might be in a bigger move.

This guide is a light, honest introduction: what the pattern is, the rules that must hold, how to spot an impulse and a correction, why counting is genuinely hard, and how CPE Trade Pro shows a light count as context. Educational content only — nothing here is financial advice.

What Elliott Wave is

The framework was developed by Ralph Nelson Elliott in the 1930s. He observed that markets advance in a five-wave sequence in the direction of the larger trend, followed by a three-wave sequence against it, and that this structure repeats on every timeframe — what is one big wave on a daily chart is a full five-wave move on an hourly chart.

That fractal quality is the heart of it. Elliott Wave is not a crystal ball; it is a way of organising a chart so you can judge whether a move looks finished or still incomplete. Used well, it is a map. Used badly, it becomes a way of forcing any chart into a preferred story.

The 5-3 structure

Every complete cycle has two halves. The motive phase is five waves labelled 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and travels with the trend. The corrective phase is three waves labelled A, B, C and travels against it. Put together they form the "5-3" pattern.

WaveTypeTypical character
1ImpulseOften weak and unnoticed; a first push off a base
2CorrectionRetraces much of wave 1; the crowd thinks the move is over
3ImpulseUsually the longest and strongest; widest participation
4CorrectionSideways, often shallower than wave 2
5ImpulseFinal push; is often the least dynamic of the three impulses
A, B, CCorrectionA three-leg pull-back; B often fools people into thinking the trend resumed

The three unbreakable rules

Amateurs count waves freely; the rules are what keep a count honest. Three observations must hold for a valid impulse — and they are the same three you check before trusting anyone's count, including your own.

  1. Wave 2 never retraces more than 100% of wave 1. If it does, the move is not an impulse and the count is wrong.
  2. Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5. It can be shorter than expected, but it cannot be the runt of the three.
  3. Wave 4 never overlaps wave 1's price territory in a standard impulse. An overlap means you are probably looking at a corrective or diagonal structure instead.
⚠️
If a rule breaks, the count dies Rules are the difference between analysis and wishful thinking. When price violates one, the honest move is to abandon that count and re-label — not to bend the rule to fit.

Identifying an impulse

An impulse is the trending half of the pattern: five waves in the direction of the larger move. The practical tell is energy — wave 3 usually shows the widest expansion and the highest volume, while waves 2 and 4 are pauses. Momentum indicators typically peak during wave 3 and then diverge as wave 5 forms.

Corrections A-B-C

After five waves, the market corrects. The simplest correction is a zigzag: a sharp A down, a smaller B bounce, then a C that usually travels about as far as A. More complex corrections take the form of flats and triangles, where price chops sideways for longer than anyone expects.

🔁 Zigzag Sharp and quick — a deep A, a shallow B, a C that roughly matches A.
⬛ Flat Sideways and overlapping, with B retracing most of A. Slower and more frustrating.
📐 Triangle Five converging legs of chop — usually a pause before the trend resumes.

Why counts are hard

Here is the honest part that most Elliott Wave material skips. Two experienced analysts can look at the same chart and produce two different counts that both respect the three rules. Until price resolves, neither is "wrong".

  • Subjectivity at every pivot. Which high is "the" wave 5? On a noisy chart, the answer is not obvious.
  • Alternate counts. Good practitioners always keep a second, invalidation-style count ready in case the first breaks.
  • Hindsight bias. Counts always look clean in a textbook and rarely so in real time, where the right-hand side of the chart does not exist yet.
  • Timeframes disagree. A completed impulse on the 15-minute chart is a small correction on the daily. Both readings can be true at once.
💡
Use the count as context, not a trigger The most useful thing a count gives you is an invalidation level — the price that would prove it wrong. That is a risk tool, not a prophecy.
